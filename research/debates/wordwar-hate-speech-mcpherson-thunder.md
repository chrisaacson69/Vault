## Argument Structures

### Aff — Octavius Thunder

1. **The category is empty.** *"Hate speech is a term that's really a social construct. It doesn't really exist."* Asked by the moderator to accept a working definition, he refuses outright and offers his own: *"my definition of hate speech is saying that you hate anything. I hate cold spaghetti."*
2. **So the resolution reduces to a triviality he already holds.** Pressed on how the First Amendment can protect something that doesn't exist: *"Hate speech is speech and speech exists. I think the first amendment should protect speech."*
3. **Who decides — the central argument.** Whoever holds the pen on the definition holds the speech right. *"It would really come down to their personal preference and whatever foreign entities they may be associated with. And they would have the full control to move the goalpost at will."*
4. **The ratchet.** *"The more laws that are passed, none are removed to balance it out. Laws are only added on"* — so hate-speech law accumulates in one direction only.
5. **The worked instance:** the 2017–2021 expansion of the official antisemitism definition to cover criticism of Israel, plus anti-BDS statutes, as proof the capture is not hypothetical. He argues he could be criminally liable for raising Larry Silverstein / 9-11 / USS Liberty claims without having said anything hateful.
6. **Against protected classes as such.** *"I think having protected categories is stupid if we're trying to promote equality"*; enhancement is insulting to the protected group, and beating someone is equally hateful regardless of the victim's class.
7. **Targeting, not content, is the line he'll accept.** Chud the Builder shouting slurs at people in the street *"makes you an [asshole]"* and warrants a beating and social consequences; the same words posted to his own channel are different, because nobody is targeted.
8. **Immutability cuts wider than the protected list.** Obesity and appearance are largely unchosen and unprotected, so the list is arbitrary — *"I think it's even more offensive to a fat person if you call them fat than an Indian person for calling them Indian."*
9. **Closing:** the definitional pen ends up with *"our satanic pedophilic overlords"* pursuing *"global enslavement."*

### Neg — Chase McPherson

1. **Adopts the UN definition** of hate speech (attacks or pejorative/discriminatory language targeting a person or group on the basis of religion, ethnicity, nationality, race, colour, descent, gender, or other identity factors).
2. **Intent is the whole framework.** Criminal liability requires a culpable mental state; identical physical acts are sorted by intent (murder vs. manslaughter). *"Criminalizing hate speech on the basis of intent is legally orthodox."*
3. **A three-part criminalization standard:** (i) intent to harass, threaten or incite; (ii) targeting a protected characteristic; (iii) a reasonable likelihood of harm. Explicitly modelled on **Brandenburg**, and offered as *"a modest doctrinal step, not a categorical leap."*
4. **Precedent:** *Wisconsin v. Mitchell* (1993, 9-0) upholding penalty enhancement for victim selection by race; California's enhancement statute and its protected-characteristic list; fighting words; defamation as the model of intent making speech actionable.
5. **The social/legal split.** Below the threshold the consequences should be social (including civil suits — Alex Jones and Sandy Hook as the example); above it, criminal. *"If you're just on Twitter saying slurs… it's not hate speech that we can really criminalize."*
6. **Protected classes are historically grounded** — slavery, Jim Crow, religious persecution as founding motive — and they run in every direction, evidenced by the Disney hiring leak and Musk's suit.
7. **A statistic on symmetry:** an Albany Law School study of the Roberts Court 2005–2014 — 7 discrimination claims brought by white claimants, 7 upheld; 11 brought by minorities, 5 upheld.
8. **Israel/Jewish distinction:** criticising a state is not criticising an ethnicity, though he concedes the line is *"a little thin… because of the ethnostate."*

### The Aff's opening — right instinct, wrong metaphysics

> **Chris:** "Aff starts out interesting but kinda steps on definitions.. that something is a **'social construct' doesn't make it not real**, but the points of defining 'hate speech' ring true — you can 'hate' things and this is not, while other things that hint at some topics may get defined as such, but not even be hate."

So [Constructed ≠ Arbitrary](../philosophy/constructed-not-arbitrary.md) holds and the Aff walks into it, exactly as the masculinity round's con did. But the verdict is split, and the split is the useful part: **the ontological claim is wrong and the boundary claim is right.** Octavius had two arguments available and reached for the weaker one. The strong version doesn't need the category to be unreal — it needs the category to be *mis-drawn in both directions at once*, which is what Chris identifies: it **over-includes** (things that merely touch a subject get classified as hate) and **under-includes** (actual hatred of things and of unprotected groups doesn't count). A definition that fails in both directions is a bad definition without any help from metaphysics, and defending it costs nothing.

*Seed 2's open half stays open:* whether the vault thesis needs a carve-out for **legally operative** constructs, where constructibility is the risk rather than a red herring, is not settled by the above — Chris ruled on the fallacy, not on the carve-out.

### The Neg's real position is about sentencing, not speech

> **Chris:** "The neg starts out with the UN definition, and **pivots to intent and just to determine punishment for another crime**. They then go on to talk about that there are already restrictions on 1A and defamation."

> **Chris:** "you nail the points on 1 and 2 very well."

Confirms seed 1's diagnosis. Chase's three-part standard is a **sentencing** framework wearing a speech-protection costume: it operates on conduct already criminal (harassment, threats, incitement, robbery) and asks what the speech reveals about mens rea. Nothing in it removes protection from speech that stands alone — he says so himself about slurs on Twitter. The resolution asks whether the First Amendment should protect hate speech; his answer, once specified, is *yes, except where it's already unprotected.*

### The enhancement objection — Chris's standing view

> **Chris:** "Aff starts getting strong here as they show these definitions **define hate as 'characteristics' and not actual hate**. I have always sided with the idea that **if you are doing a crime to someone, you probably 'hate' them for some reason**, so putting on an 'addition'.. does it really matter about battery if you hate them or not?"

This is the round's best available argument and only the Aff gestures at it. The charge is that the enhancement is **misnamed**: it does not key on hatred, it keys on the *victim's membership in a listed category*. A beating delivered out of genuine personal hatred toward an unlisted victim carries no enhancement; a beating delivered coldly against a listed victim does. So either the statute is not really about hate — in which case the moral premise advertising it is doing no work — or it is about hate and is radically under-inclusive. Octavius states the intuition (*"if you go and beat the [hell] out of someone, you hate them… you're going to get a lesser sentence because they're the same race as you"*) and never converts it into the dilemma.

### Where the Aff narrowed himself

### The doctrine both debaters got wrong

> **Chris:** "It is frustrating, the discussion on what 1A doesn't allow, as these are inaccurate.. **'fighting words' are about the only exception**, the trope 'fire in a theater' **is protected** — the actual case was inciting a stampede on a town street. And in most of these cases, **the crime is not 'speech' but the results of that speech**. 'Fire in a theater' is not punishable if nothing happens as a result."

The structural point is right and is the thing the round needed: **most of these doctrines punish a result or a course of conduct, not an utterance**, which is why "hate speech" as a free-standing category has no natural home in them. Chase's own framework quietly concedes it — everything he wants criminalised is already criminal for non-speech reasons.

*Ground-truth pass, because this page will be published:*

- **"Fire in a theatre" is dicta, and Chris is right that it is not a holding about theatres.** It comes from Holmes in *Schenck v. United States* (1919), whose actual facts were **leaflets urging resistance to the draft** — not a theatre and, as far as I can source, not a street stampede either. **The stampede detail I cannot confirm and have not written into the page as fact**; if you have the case in mind, name it and I'll add it. (There is a real historical panic-death incident — the 1913 Italian Hall disaster — which may be what the memory is attached to, but that is an event, not a case.)
- **The test Holmes announced there is no longer the law.** *Brandenburg v. Ohio* (1969) replaced clear-and-present-danger with **directed to inciting imminent lawless action *and* likely to produce it** — which is the standard Chase actually cited, correctly, in his opening.
- **"About the only exception" is too strong** as written. The recognised unprotected categories include incitement, true threats, fighting words, obscenity, child sexual abuse material, defamation, fraud, and speech integral to criminal conduct. But the point that survives — and is the Aff's entire case in one line — is that **hate speech is not among them**, which the Supreme Court has said about as plainly as it says anything (*Matal v. Tam*, 2017).
- **The two cases that would have decided this round, and neither man names either.** *Wisconsin v. Mitchell* (1993, 9-0) upholds penalty enhancement — Chase cites it and it is his best authority. ***R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*** (1992) strikes down a bias-motivated speech ordinance as viewpoint-discriminatory — which is Octavius's best authority and he has never heard of it. **Together they draw exactly the line the debate was groping for:** you may enhance a sentence for a *criminal act* selected by bias, and you may not criminalise the *expression* by category. That is the whole answer, it is settled, and an hour was spent circling it.

### The domain import — protected classes belong to a different body of law

> **Chris:** "people often get the 1st amendment exceptions wrong, and **the civil rights movement had little to nothing to do with this**.. so trying to tie this to 'protected classes' is absurd."

This is the deepest structural criticism available against the Neg's case, and it explains why the round's long discrimination tangent went nowhere: **the whole tangent was conducted in the wrong body of law.**

Two separate lineages are being welded together as if they were one:

| | Where "protected classes" come from | Where First Amendment exceptions come from |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | The Fourteenth Amendment's equal-protection jurisprudence and the civil-rights statutes built on it (Civil Rights Act 1964, Title VII, Fair Housing Act) | Free-speech doctrine developed on its own line: fighting words (*Chaplinsky*, 1942), incitement (*Brandenburg*, 1969), obscenity (*Miller*, 1973), defamation (*NYT v. Sullivan*, 1964), true threats |
| **What it governs** | How the **state and regulated actors may treat people** — employment, housing, public accommodation, sentencing | What **speech the state may punish** |
| **Role of category membership** | Constitutive — the whole doctrine is about listed characteristics | **Absent.** No unprotected category is defined by the victim's group |

So Chase's move — protected classes exist, they are historically justified, therefore speech targeting them may be criminalised — is a **domain import**, and the import is the unargued step. Everything he says about Jim Crow, the Disney hiring leak, and the Albany Law School numbers is *about discrimination law*, where it is on point, and none of it establishes anything about what speech loses protection.

**And the point is not merely arguable — it is the holding of the case neither debater named.** *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul* (1992) struck down an ordinance targeting bias-motivated expression precisely because it singled out speech by the *category of its target*, which is viewpoint discrimination even within a class of otherwise-unprotected speech. Read against *Wisconsin v. Mitchell* (1993) — where enhancement survives because it attaches to **conduct**, not expression — the pair says exactly what Chris says: you may use category membership in **sentencing an act**, and you may not use it to **define an exception to speech protection**. The vault's third-man read and the controlling doctrine converge, and an hour was spent without either debater reaching it.

*This also retro-explains the Aff's instinct.* Octavius's *"having protected categories is stupid if we're trying to promote equality"* is a blunt version of the same objection, aimed at the wrong target — he attacks protected classes as such, where the available attack is on their **transplantation into speech law**. The categories can be entirely legitimate where they live and still be foreign to the First Amendment question.

### "Stochastic terrorism"

> **Chris:** "and ugg.. NOOOO!!! 'stochastic terrorism' — another garbage concept! Somehow someone saying something in passing is now responsible for another person to act on their own for something related.. **how far away do we have to get from causality to keep accountability?** :)"

Note who introduced it: **the moderator**, unprompted, as a gloss on the Aff's position. The objection is a causal-attenuation one and it is the same instrument as the vault's [single-variate](./wordwar-feminism-rerun-david-tareyak.md) complaint from the previous round, pointed at responsibility rather than explanation: an intervening agent who deliberates and chooses is normally treated as *breaking* the causal chain, not transmitting it. Stochastic-terrorism reasoning keeps the chain intact across that break, which is what makes it unfalsifiable — any speech is upstream of *some* actor, so the attribution can never fail. Chris's question is the right test to put to it: **name the distance at which accountability stops**, and if no distance can be named, the concept isn't doing causal work.

## Vault position on the resolution

Stated at the close of the discussion, and worth separating from the round verdict because the two point opposite ways.

> **Chris:** "I personally think **1A should protect 'hate speech'** for all the problems it has with definitions and enforcement, and that **the idea that somehow it makes a crime worse is absurd**.. but **the neg was the better debater this round**."

So the vault affirms the resolution, on two grounds and neither of them the Aff's:

1. **Not because the category is unreal, but because it is unadministrable.** The definitional failure runs in both directions at once (over-including speech that merely touches a subject, under-including real hatred toward unlisted groups), and the enforcement failure follows from it — a category that cannot be drawn cleanly cannot be applied predictably, and whoever holds the pen can move it. This keeps [Constructed ≠ Arbitrary](../philosophy/constructed-not-arbitrary.md) intact: the objection is to the **construction being bad**, not to its being a construction.
2. **The enhancement is misnamed.** It keys on the victim's listed category rather than on hatred, so it is either not about hate at all or radically under-inclusive — and it is *additionally* a domain import, borrowing equal-protection machinery to do First Amendment work.

**A clean is/ought separation is worth logging alongside it.** Presented with the recognised unprotected categories — fighting words, incitement, obscenity, defamation, true threats — Chris's response is *"I disagree with it, but I agree that is what is considered law."* The descriptive question (what the doctrine holds) and the normative one (what it should hold) are kept apart, which is exactly what neither debater managed: the Neg argued a policy he wanted as though it were the doctrine, and the Aff argued a doctrine he liked as though it settled the policy.

**And the round verdict runs against the vault's own position.** The side the vault thinks is right lost the debate, on execution — see the [ledger](./word-war-debate-series.md#predictions-ledger) and the Garcia comparison.

## Open Questions

1. **The "stampede" case — searched for and not found; closed as unsourced.** Chris's core correction stands and is on the page: *"fire in a crowded theatre"* was never a holding, it is Holmes's dicta in *Schenck* (whose facts were anti-draft leaflets), and the test it announced was superseded by *Brandenburg*. The remembered detail — a commotion started when horses panicked at a gunshot or loud noise — **could not be sourced by either of us**, and is therefore deliberately absent from the page rather than hedged into it. Recorded here so the search isn't repeated from scratch.

   *Near-miss worth noting, offered as a doctrine rather than as the missing case:* the classic panic-in-a-crowd fact pattern in the common law is ***Scott v. Shepherd*** (1773), the **squib case** — a lit firework thrown into a market, passed on in self-preservation by two intervening people, injuring a third. Different facts, no horses, and not a speech case. It is flagged because it is the authority for the question Chris actually raises two sections below: **how far a chain of intervening free choices can run before the originator stops being liable.** If the memory is attached to anything, a causation case is a likelier ancestor than a First Amendment one.
2. **Does the enhancement dilemma deserve promotion?** The argument is portable and neither debater built it: a "hate crime" enhancement keys not on hatred but on the **victim's membership in a listed category**, so either it isn't about hate (and its moral advertising is doing no work) or it is about hate and is radically under-inclusive — genuine personal hatred toward an unlisted victim carries no enhancement. Natural home would be [Legal Theory](../philosophy/morality/legal-theory/README.md); it also rhymes with [aggregation vs. sorting](../philosophy/morality/legal-theory/aggregation-vs-sorting.md) (a rule that sorts by category rather than by the thing it names).
3. **Is "agree on the proposition, disagree on an adjacent policy" a distinct failure mode?** It is not the [verbal dispute](./wordwar-too-sensitive-guptill-tejada.md) — there the two asserted the same claim under opposed labels. Here both endorse the same rule about speech and then fight about **sentencing**. The resolution is not contested; it is *vacated*, and the round refills the hour with a neighbouring question. Third species, or a variant of [topic-talk vs. resolution-work](./word-war-debate-series.md#cross-round-findings)?
4. **Promote the etymology pair to finding 1?** The bracket now has the same intervention from two moderators on opposite sides of the attribution rule — Pisco blocking an etymological move and **owning it**, Kyla blocking one and framing it as a request for clarification. That is a sharper test of where the line sits than either round alone, and finding 1's open successor asks exactly this.
5. **Causal attenuation as a standing instrument.** Chris's test for stochastic-terrorism reasoning — *name the distance at which accountability stops; if none can be named, the concept isn't doing causal work* — generalises past this round to any attribution across an intervening deliberating agent. Worth a home, or keep as a specimen line here?
