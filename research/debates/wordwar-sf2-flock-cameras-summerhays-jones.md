## ⭐ The evidentiary double standard, and the ledger it produces

Summerhays builds the burden into his opening, before either case is heard:

> *"He has to prove that the American public has **tangibly been harmed** by the use of flock cameras thus far. Because Rex comes from the Candace conspiracy crowd, he's going to assume my reasons are speculative. **My reasons are logical entailments**… He needs the quantity of material events to validate his arguments. That's why he's a reactionary… **I just need to use logic.** And you are welcome to reject logic, but I don't know if that would look great optically."*

**One standard for the opponent — realized events — and another for himself — entailment.** Jones tests it immediately, and it collapses inside four minutes:

> **Jones:** *"Do you have proof of this, or is it just a concept?"* … *"These cameras are used to capture hundreds of millions of images. Wouldn't you have use cases?"*
> **Summerhays:** ***"No, I don't have it."***
> **Jones:** *"Don't you have stats on how these cameras are so beneficial to America?"*
> **Summerhays:** ***"Not yet."*** … *"I'm using primary arguments. Like I said, **I don't need material events to have happened.**"*

Then Jones supplies the thing Summerhays demanded — a realized harm, with a mechanism:

> *"A police officer was arrested for stalking. They then had to arrest the officer assigned to investigate him, because that officer was also abusing the system to stalk people."*
> **Summerhays:** ***"Yeah, that sounds like an edge case."***

**So the ledger, on a resolution written in the present tense about a technology already deployed at national scale:**

| | Realized benefit | Realized harm |
|---|---|---|
| **Aff produced** | **none** — *"not yet"* | — |
| **Neg produced** | — | documented abuse, plus the investigator abusing it too |

**⭐ Third specimen of the same losing move, and it is time to name it.** [Tejeda in QF C](./wordwar-r3-iran-beneficial-tejeda-jones.md) claimed future benefits while refusing to price present costs. [Charsky in QF D](./wordwar-r3-nationalism-socialism-summerhays-charsky.md) held that market failure is structural and inescapable while state failure is a solvable design problem. Summerhays here demands material proof of harm and offers entailment for benefit. **Different content, one shape: a demanding evidentiary standard for the opponent's side of the ledger and a permissive one for your own.** In every case the opponent noticed, and in every case it was the round's turning point.

### And the pre-buttal misfired, expensively

He spent his opening characterising Jones' *method* — *"reactionary," "needs a material event to start the thinking process," "the Candace conspiracy crowd."* Jones then opened with **a Supreme Court holding, the text of the Fourth Amendment, and Blackstone via Franklin.**

**The pre-buttal did not merely miss — it committed its author to the anti-empirical side of a debate in which he then had no empirics**, and handed Jones the framing that *he* was the one arguing from law and record. **Pre-butting an opponent's method is a bet on your read of him**, and this is what losing that bet costs.

## ⭐⭐ *Chatrie* — the best single piece of preparation in the bracket

Jones' opening cites a decision from ten weeks before the debate:

> *"In **Chatrie v. United States**, June 29th 2026, the Supreme Court ruled **6–3** that law enforcement requests for historical location data — Google location history via geofence warrants — **constitute a search under the Fourth Amendment**… individuals maintain a reasonable expectation of privacy in their detailed location history, and collection requires a warrant **regardless of the short time span or small quantity of the data gathered**. **Location data is part of a person's private effects.** The government cannot obtain those from a third party without a warrant. That's Justice Neil Gorsuch."*

⚠ *Recorded as cited; this page cannot independently verify the ruling, and it does not need to — **Summerhays never contests its existence, its accuracy, or its applicability.***

**Two things make it decisive rather than merely impressive:**

1. **It pre-refutes the Aff's own factual framing.** Summerhays' defence is that these are *"just still images, not running footage"* — and Jones points out the Court **specifically rejected** the government's fallback that a search is acceptable because it pulled only *"a narrow time-limited slice of a larger data set."* The exact move, already ruled on.
2. **It converts the resolution.** *Beneficial or harmful* becomes *lawful or not*, and the Neg only has to hold the second.

Jones then reads the Amendment aloud and lands on the operative word — *"persons, houses, papers, and **effects**"* — the same word in the holding.

### The Aff's answer, in three stages, ending somewhere he cannot come back from

1. **Misread it:** *"you cited the Fourth Amendment, which is private property, your houses."*
2. **Diminish it:** *"Heavy on the word **opinion**. Heavy on the usage of opinion."*
3. **Reject it:** ***"Yes, I disagree… I'm allowed to disagree. I disagree with the Supreme Court justices' quote. That happens all the time."***

**In front of a constitutionalist audience, the Affirmative's settled position is that he disagrees with a controlling Supreme Court holding on the Fourth Amendment.** It is not a slip — it is his considered fallback after two failed attempts, and Jones closes it: *"my whole point is that your position is **anti-American**. Your position violates the Constitution. You say you're fine with that."*

**⭐ Note what that is.** In [QF A](./wordwar-r3-dsa-medeiros-bourdeau.md), *"unamerican"* was Medeiros' **unargued terminal value** — it worked because the audience already held it, and Bourdeau's demand that he ground it changed nothing. Here Jones makes the identical charge **with a citation behind it**. Same move, argued version. This is the round that shows the difference is available.

## ⭐⭐ The trap the Aff's own premise sets for him

Summerhays' entire case rests on one distinction: *public space carries no warrant requirement.* Perez asks him whether the public may therefore have the data.

> **Jones:** *"Would it be okay for absolutely anyone to access that information? Should every single flock camera be on a dedicated URL for anyone to look at at any time?"*
> **Summerhays:** ***"No. No, but that is not the way it is currently set up. But that would be wrong."***
> **Jones:** *"But if it were public information, why would they **not** have a right to it? **You're the taxpayer. You pay for those flock cameras.** I own that information. You don't have any reason for it through warrants — it's public."*
> **Summerhays:** *"…your random person cannot access these databases, and that was kind of my whole point. **It's only law enforcement that can access these things.**"*

**That is the case conceding itself.** If the information is genuinely public, the public may have it. If only law enforcement may have it, it is not public — it is a **law-enforcement-privileged compilation**, which is the thing the Fourth Amendment calls a search. Summerhays says *"that was kind of my whole point"* while stating the premise's refutation.

**The cleanest logical kill in the bracket**, and it costs Jones one question.

## Asked three times to bound his principle, the Aff widens it instead

| Perez / Jones asks | Summerhays answers |
|---|---|
| China's predictive policing, total information awareness, social credit — *"how do you ensure that's not the next step?"* | *"CCTV can be used to track people at any point in public. And **I wouldn't even have a huge problem with that**, because it is in public"* |
| Flock sharing data with private companies like FedEx | *"I **wasn't necessarily aware** of it, but **I don't mind it**"* |
| Guarding against political persecution and selective prosecution | *"We would have to have a **separate conversation**"* — then: *"Rex hasn't demonstrated that there's a problem on mass, so it's meaningless to scheme up regulations before the problem has been demonstrated"* |

**Every invitation to draw a limit is answered by extending the principle.** It is the mirror image of the [stretched term](./wordwar-r2-abortion-vic-thunder.md) problem: there, a debater needed his key phrase to be wide and narrow at once and hid the boundary; here, Summerhays refuses a boundary altogether, so *"public means fair game"* swallows CCTV, social credit and corporate data-sharing — and the audience is invited to accept all of it.

## Blackstone rejected on the record, twice, under maximum pressure

> **Summerhays:** *"I disagree that it's better for a hundred guilty people to escape than one person to be punished. That sounds absurd."*

Jones makes it concrete, and loads the hypothetical with **the group this audience most wants removed**:

> *"Let's say we've got a hundred illegal immigrants, and one innocent person who's going to be pulled over because someone fat-fingered something in the flock system. They're going to have a gun pointed at their head. Would you say those hundred illegals — it's worth getting them out if it means we point that gun at one person's head?"*
> **Summerhays:** ***"I would say one person being falsely accused is less harmful than a hundred criminals being released. Yeah, absolutely."***

Maximum incentive to take the easy exit, and he takes the hard line twice. **Blackstone's ratio is close to sacred in the constitutionalist tradition this pool inhabits**, and he rejects it explicitly, in a round where his opponent has already tied it to Franklin and the founding.

## The Neg's closing is the best argued in the tournament

Jones re-reads the Fourth Amendment *"for posterity,"* re-cites *Chatrie*, and runs the parity again — *we could allow door-kicking and compulsory blood collection to clear cold cases; how is Flock different?* Then the argument that has no answer in this room:

> ⭐ *"Maybe you could say it's beneficial. **But you have to say that your side is going to be in charge forever.** And that's not how the American system works. The American system of government **is designed to be inefficient**, designed to have change take place slowly — precisely because if you give the government authoritarian controls… **even if your side does it for the right reasons, it opens up the door to the other side using it for the wrong reasons.**"*

**That is the rotation-of-power argument — build no instrument you would not hand to your enemy — and it is the most serious thing anyone has said in this bracket.** It is also a genuine **turn**: it takes Summerhays' *"the people we already trust"* premise and converts trust in the current government into an argument against the tool.

*"1984 — and the answer to 1984 has been and will always be 1776."*

⚠ **And then he spoils the register.** *"You hate America just like your master Andrew Wilson."* An ad hominem bolted to a winning closing, and the second time in three rounds he has [inflated a strong narrow argument into a weaker broad one](./word-war-debate-series.md#cross-round-findings).

## The Aff's closing is his best work, and almost all of it is new

Summerhays finally supplies the positive case — gas-station theft and stolen purses, businesses that *"cannot pick up and leave,"* the permit and jurisdiction structure, business customers being able to access only their own data, an offer to *"audit Flock as a company,"* and the independent-auditor rage-bait scenario. Plus the framing he should have opened on: *"we live and drive to work with an understanding every day that we are being watched. **This is why we put on clothes in the morning.**"*

**Three problems, in ascending order of cost:**

1. ⚠ **Almost all of it is new in the closing** — the **third consecutive round** in this bracket to feature substantial new argument in a closing, and Jones has no chance to answer it.
2. *"I wish we could have got more into the substance of the positive case, **but Rex didn't want to go there**"* — he was asked for it directly at 13:04 and answered *"no, I don't have it."*
3. **The two closing lines are aimed at the wrong room.** *"The bad guys would be anti-Flock"* is the nothing-to-hide argument in its purest form; and *"This new logic primarily serves Democrats, so why is Rex using it?"* asks a pool that has spent the whole bracket voting anti-establishment to treat privacy as a partisan tell.

## Perez's seventh round, and she is still the format's best instrument

Symmetric and biting again, and one of her questions is the round's most useful:

- **To Summerhays:** China's expansion of the same technology into predictive policing and social credit — *"how do you ensure that's not going to be the next step here?"*
- **To Jones:** ⭐ *"Constitutional opinions can change. **Regardless of the rulings**, do you feel the net benefit of preventing this is there?"* — the right question, because it takes his best weapon away and makes him argue the resolution rather than the law. He answers it well (foreign compromise of a single credentialed officer; no two-factor authentication; the documented stalking arrests).
- **To Summerhays:** political persecution and selective punishment under total information awareness.
- **To Jones:** the **exculpatory** value — proving your own innocence from public data. He answers on the Ninth Amendment and unenumerated rights.

## ⭐⭐ The safeguard is "my side wins forever" — stated, tested, conceded, and kept

Chris: *"Admits that there is no defense against corruption, relying on **winning elections** to stop it!"* Verified, and it goes further than that. Jones runs the [rotation-of-power argument](#the-negs-closing-is-the-best-argued-in-the-tournament) as a live cross-examination and Summerhays walks every step of it:

> **Summerhays:** *"**We are going to win infinite elections if we win the next one and you'll never have to vote again.**"*
> **Jones:** *"If the Democrats get in with the powers you've granted them, could they abuse the flock system?"* — **"They could. That's why I'm trying to make sure that doesn't happen."**
> **Jones:** *"Are you going to be able to make sure Republicans win every election?"* — **"No. You can only try."**
> **Jones:** *"You agree there's a national database? So the Democrats would get access to that same database?"* — **"They would. We would be screwed either way."**
> **Summerhays:** *"**I'm giving the keys to the kingdom to my party right now, because our party is the federal government.**"* … and on there never being another Democratic administration: *"**God willing, inshallah.**"*

**So the entire safeguard against abuse is that his own side holds power permanently — and he concedes, in sequence, that he cannot guarantee it, that the other side would inherit the same database, and that everyone would then be *"screwed either way."* He maintains the position anyway.** Jones' answer is the round's cleanest statement of principle:

> *"I say the American government should not have this power over the American people **regardless of who's in charge**. I don't care. It's about the principles, the rights, the Bill of Rights, the Constitution. **You care about a side winning.**"*

And the factual floor gives way at the same time. Asked whether every officer with access reaches the same national system, or whether he simply does not know: **"I don't know."**

## ⭐⭐ The load-bearing word is *"flock cameras"* — a device, or a network?

Chris found the equivocation the whole round runs on, and it is in the Aff's closing:

> *"He immediately goes to **private cameras as 'good'**, and then uses that to justify police use. I think he doesn't get the point that **private cameras are not interconnected while flock cameras are**, and this is where it becomes abuse."*

**That is the round's real definitional axis, and neither man names it.** Summerhays' case is built on a **device** reading — still images, permits, a business's own property, *"business customers can only access their own data,"* *"these are all places that already had legal recourse to record you on their private property before flock cameras."* Every one of those claims is true of *a camera*. Jones' case is built on a **network** reading — a national, searchable, cross-agency index with no two-factor authentication.

**The Aff's argument only works on the device reading and his opponent's only works on the network reading, and the network reading is what Flock sells.** A shop's camera is a local artifact whose reach is bounded by the cost of asking for the footage; an interconnected index removes that cost. **The equivocation is: *businesses already record you* (true, uncontroversial) → *therefore Flock is fine* (false, because Flock's product is the interconnection, not the recording).**

It is the same **referential** load-bearing problem as *"the DSA"* in [QF A](./wordwar-r3-dsa-medeiros-bourdeau.md) — which object does the name pick out? — and this time **the Neg wins the round without ever pinning it**, asserting the network reading rather than establishing it. Had Summerhays defended the device reading explicitly and forced Jones to prove the interconnection, the round is much closer.

## ⚠ The doctrine both men needed, and the case the Neg names and drops

Chris: *"He tries to tie 4A to only private property and it is hands off for public property… no. **Privacy is not about consent, it is about reasonable expectations.**"*

That is the correction, and it has a name the round never properly uses. The Fourth Amendment **protects people, not places** — the property framing Summerhays leans on has not been the governing test for decades, and *"anywhere in public is fair game"* is not what the doctrine says. What makes aggregated public movement a search is that the *whole* of a person's movements reveals what no individual observation does.

**And Jones has the case.** At 19:32 he says ***"Carpenter — uh, Chatrie"*** and switches immediately to the newer ruling. **Carpenter is the one that answers Summerhays' public/private framing head-on**, and he abandons it inside two words in favour of the case that answers the *"just still images"* framing. He needed both: one for each of his opponent's two defences.

**On plain view**, Chris's read is right — *"mostly right, wrong on a couple of examples."* An officer lawfully positioned may act on what is in plain sight; that is real, and Summerhays' window example roughly tracks it. What does **not** follow is the inference he builds on it: **plain view licenses observation by someone lawfully present, not systematic automated collection by a machine that is always present.** Which is Chris's crux —

> *"The Aff has a good point that police can sit and observe, but **I think automating this is the crux of the issue**."*

**Correct, and it is the argument that would have decided the round on the merits.** A stakeout is self-limiting because it costs an officer's time; that cost is the practical privacy protection, and automation removes it. Neither man states it. *(Parked for discussion below.)*

## Smaller corrections and confirmations from Chris

- **The "public" framing does not reach the conclusion.** *"He describes the cameras as 'public' items that can be used to assist law enforcement, so I am not seeing the justification for government to use these."* Right: private devices lawfully recording public space establishes nothing about a **government** entitlement to query a **national index** of the results. And the school example contradicts it directly — the data is *"only available to the police,"* which is the same self-refutation as the [public-URL trap](#-the-trap-the-affs-own-premise-sets-for-him).
- **Safeguards cannot be deferred past deployment.** On *"we would have to have a separate conversation"* about regulating abuse: *"if we can't get benefits or create harm via corruption, **this needs to be decided now, not after it is implemented**."* The sequencing is the point — a safeguard designed after the database exists is a safeguard on a database that already exists.
- **The China answer is weak.** *"Full video vs. rapid still"* is a difference of degree in the collection medium, not of kind in the capability, and it does not touch the predictive-policing question that was asked.
- ⭐ **His positions are consistent across rounds, which is the problem.** *"Summerhays doesn't like the innocent burden. This tracks with his nationalism beliefs — he has no problem with a highly authoritarian state."* The man who told Perez [*"I don't reject national socialism"* and *"inherently authoritarian maybe"*](./wordwar-r3-nationalism-socialism-summerhays-charsky.md) is the same man rejecting Blackstone's ratio here. **This is not drift; it is a coherent worldview showing up twice** — and it is coherent in a direction that costs him with this pool.
- **The Wilson dig may also be baseless.** *"I am not sure Summerhays is even affiliated with him."* So the ad hominem is not merely crude but possibly unfounded — worse than the [inflation problem](./word-war-debate-series.md#cross-round-findings) already logged against Jones, since inflation at least starts from something true.
- ⭐ **Second consecutive pre-written, non-responsive closing.** Chris on QF D: *"a prewritten close that ties socialism to globalism and is basically a null."* Here: *"it feels this closing was prepared as it doesn't directly address the debate."* **A contender-level habit, not a one-off** — and it interacts badly with [defect 15](./word-war-debate-series.md#format-defects-and-their-cheap-fixes): if the last word is free, a *pre-written* last word spends it on material the round has already moved past.

## ⚠ And the six-hour window may decide it regardless

Chris: *"Neg has the better arguments here and hopefully this plus his reach will carry — though **the 6 hour voting window might mean anything goes!**"*

The right worry, and it is the [confirmed dilution result](./word-war-debate-series.md#-finding-11s-compression-prediction--rescued-because-it-was-being-tested-against-the-wrong-variable) pointing at its own limit. A window cut from 48 hours to 24 to **6** does not merely shrink the pool — it shifts *who* is in it, from *people who watched the debate* to *people who were online when it dropped*. At that size the ballot measures **list speed**, and the relationship between argument quality and outcome gets noisier in both directions. **A six-hour window is not a smaller sample of the same electorate; it is a different electorate.**

## ⭐ Crystallized — [The Slack in the Law](../philosophy/morality/legal-theory/slack-in-the-law.md)

The automation question below was parked for discussion and Chris took it up the same day, producing a thesis that is **portable past this round**: *enforcement has always been rationed by cost, and the gap between what the law forbids and what is punished is where liberty has been living — automation removes the rationing without changing a word of the code.*

Its core claim is a two-knob table: **thick code + expensive enforcement** is where we live and is tolerable *because* the slack absorbs the excess; **thin code + cheap enforcement** is the libertarian ambition and is *"next door to complete totalitarianism"* (Chris); and **thick code + cheap enforcement** is the empty cell no society has ever occupied and a surveillance layer now makes reachable. The page also carries the **deterrence vs. prevention** distinction behind Chris's *"we could build cars that don't speed, but is that really reasonable?"*, the red-light-camera precedent and its **confrontation-clause** objection, and the observation that **automation does not enforce the law but the machine-legible subset of it**.

**This round is its dated specimen.** Everything below is the raw material.

## Parked for discussion — the automation question

Chris: *"the aff has a good point that police can sit and observe, but I think automating this is the crux of the issue **which we will discuss later if it doesn't get covered**."* It did not get covered. The question worth having on its own terms:

**If an officer may lawfully watch you cross a public square, what changes when a machine watches every square, always, and remembers?** Candidate answers the round never reached — that the *cost* of surveillance was itself the historical protection and automation removes it; that aggregation produces a *qualitatively* different object (a life, not an observation); that retention converts a present observation into a permanent record queryable against future suspicion; and that the constitutional test has never been *"could a person have seen this?"* but *"what expectation is reasonable?"*

## Who argued better

**Rex Jones, and it is the most one-sided round in the bracket on substance** — which is strange to write about a round between two undefeated contenders, and stranger still given [QF C](./wordwar-r3-iran-beneficial-tejeda-jones.md) already held that title for him.

Applying [finding 19](./word-war-debate-series.md#cross-round-findings)'s check, the strongest version of the Aff's case is real and was available: *ALPR data is collected in public where no reasonable expectation of privacy has traditionally attached; the same logic already licenses traffic stops, tint laws and public-nudity prosecution; retention limits and audit requirements are a policy question, not a constitutional one; and a technology deployed for two years with no mass-scale documented abuse is not obviously harmful.* **Summerhays had every piece of that** — the public/private construction in his opening is genuinely well-built — **and lost it by demanding evidence he could not himself supply, refusing every limiting principle, and finishing on "I disagree with the Supreme Court."**

## Open Questions

- **Does a Supreme Court citation travel to a lay ballot?** The [inventory](./formal-debate-concepts.md) says judge-tuned moves invert under an audience vote. *Chatrie* is the opposite case — a **legible** technical weapon, since "the Supreme Court ruled this unconstitutional ten weeks ago" needs no training to follow. If Jones wins big, that is evidence the format rewards *authority* even where it punishes *technique*.
- **Is the evidentiary double standard now a finding?** Three specimens across three consecutive rounds, each one the round's turning point. It wants promoting and a name.
- **Does rejecting Blackstone cost anything measurable?** It should be lethal with this pool, but [the margin is unrecoverable](./word-war-debate-series.md#-round-3-resolved--the-final-four-is-set-and-the-results-page-has-been-wiped-2026-09-08), so the question may never resolve.
- **Three consecutive rounds with new arguments in a closing.** The format gives the last word to a speaker nobody can answer, and contenders have worked out that it is free. That is a [defect](./word-war-debate-series.md#format-defects-and-their-cheap-fixes) and it now has enough specimens to log as one.
