# Chris vs. a Deontologist — "One Should Not Torture Unicorns"
> Chris runs the unicorn line ("no agents ⟹ nothing to be right or wrong about"). The deontologist answers with a *conditional* universal and borrows priors to apply it. The exchange slides from **existence** to **knowledge**, and the universal he keeps turns out to be about suffering, not unicorns.

**Date:** about 2026-10-13 (recounted by Chris on 2026-10-20, "a week back")
**Source:** Chris's account in session. **No transcript.** Quotes below are Chris's paraphrase, not verbatim.
**Participants:** Chris vs. an unnamed deontologist
**Links:** [Chris vs. Mullally](./chris-mullally-callin.md) (origin of the unicorn line), [Chris vs. Claude — Non-Uniqueness](./claude-nonuniqueness-moral-grounding.md) (the Anne Frank / maxim-description exchange, `[19–20]`), [Constitutive Middle — Dialectics](../philosophy/morality/constitutive-middle-dialectics.md), [Relational Objectivity](../philosophy/epistemology/relational-objectivity.md) (Rand's "of value to whom and for what?"), [Morality Framework](../philosophy/morality/README.md), [Deontology vs. Consequentialism](../philosophy/morality/deontology-consequentialism.md)

## The arc

1. **Chris:** morals are not absolute; they need an agent and something real. Killing a human can't be unjust if there are no humans. The deontologist rejects this outright.
2. **Chris:** "one must feed unicorns." The deontologist calls it weak, but offers *"one should not torture unicorns"* as a universal.
3. **Chris:** we don't know what a unicorn is, so we don't know whether it *can* be tortured.
4. **Deontologist, appealing to priors:** we both basically know what a unicorn is (horse-like, one horn). The meanings don't match 1:1, but there's enough overlap to rule out dragons. We know not to torture animals, so it's universal that we shouldn't torture unicorns.
5. **Chris extracts a concession:** on a reasonable reading unicorns are *magical*, so we don't know whether they feel pain. The deontologist dismisses it.
6. **Chris, afterward:** "what else can we do? Everything is new. Can we dismiss everything due to uncertainty?" The priors move fits his own *morality is a probability game*. Does it reach his claim about the non-existent?

## Analysis

**1. The argument drifted from existence to knowledge.** Chris opened with *nothing there ⟹ no moral fact*. By step 3 the issue was *can't know ⟹ can't judge*. Priors answer the second claim, not the first, so the original claim was never refuted. **Recall trap:** don't follow the drift. Bring it back to "nothing there."

**2. His universal is a conditional over a property.** Spelled out: *for any being that can suffer, don't torture it; unicorns probably can.* The duty attaches to **capacity to suffer**, which is a real property of real beings. Unicorns are covered only on loan, by analogy with horses. The step-5 concession admits the duty depends on that property actually being present, which is Chris's position. A conditional over an empty class is vacuously true ("all unicorns are purple"), so granting it costs nothing. **Line:** *"I'll grant the conditional. It's about suffering, not unicorns, and suffering only exists where something can suffer."* This is the [cyanide parity](../philosophy/epistemology/relational-objectivity.md) move: "cyanide is poisonous to unicorns" has no truth value until there's a unicorn physiology.

**3. The priors move helps Chris's framework, not his.** Applying a rule by analogy is induction, and induction can be overturned. A rule that needs priors before it can be applied is not *applied* absolutely. It also leaves deontology's home ground: "animals feel pain, unicorns resemble animals" is empirical reasoning, not reasoning from duty alone.

**4. Uncertainty doesn't mean anything goes.** The vault's stance ([moral foundations](./moral-foundations-wilson-whatever.md)) is that missing certainty doesn't make every option equally good. Two supports:
- **The costs of error are lopsided.** Sparing a being that can't feel pain costs almost nothing. Torturing one that can costs a lot. Expected value gives his conclusion without an absolute.
- **Priors have failed in both directions.** Sometimes a real similarity was denied: Descartes' animal-automata, and infants operated on without anesthesia into the 1980s. Sometimes a false similarity was asserted. The error each time was a prior that never got updated, not the use of a prior. So the right response is to keep updating priors, not to discard them.

## Categorical, subdivided — the question re-asked

Chris connected this to the Anne Frank case: *if categorical imperatives must be subdivided this finely, when do they stop differing from conditional ones?* **This was asked before**, as [Non-Uniqueness `[19–20]`](./claude-nonuniqueness-moral-grounding.md), on 2026-10-10. The answer then:

- **Two axes, not one.** *Specificity of content* ("lie to *this* guard") is a different axis from *source of bindingness* (does the ought depend on wanting something?). Subdividing moves along the first axis and never touches the second. A rule about one act can still bind regardless of what you want.
- **What collapses is the test, not the distinction.** Universalizability stops filtering anything once maxims can be made arbitrarily specific (the maxim-description problem). O'Neill's reply: a maxim is your *actual reason*, not any true description of the act. The endpoint of unlimited subdivision is Dancy's **particularism**.
- **Chris's own view is functionally categorical.** It is conditional on *being an agent*, which every possible addressee meets.

**What the unicorn case adds:** subdivision ends at **properties**. The refined rule is *for anything with P, don't do X* (P = can suffer; P = is using my honesty to hunt an innocent). The rule can be categorical while **whether P holds** is empirical and uncertain. So:

| Level | Deontologist's claim | Chris's claim | Who's right |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rule form** (the conditional) | universal, binding on every agent | conceded; "functionally categorical" | both |
| **Instantiation** (is there a P-bearer?) | silent | no P-bearer ⟹ no fact | Chris |
| **Application** (does *this* thing have P?) | "we know" | a probability judgment | Chris |

**Where the equivocation charge is fair:** carrying the rule's certainty over to its application. "The conditional is universal" is true. "So we *know* not to torture unicorns" doesn't follow, because the second sentence borrows the certainty of the first. That slide is the thing to name, and step 4 is an example of it.

**This is also Mullally's split.** "Constant principle, varying application" ([Mullally, last open question](./chris-mullally-callin.md)) is the same table, with application named as the varying part. On this reading Chris and the refined deontologist agree about form and differ about (a) what *grounds* the conditional (agent structure vs. bare duty) and (b) the cases where the maxim fails even though nothing bad happens (Kant: wrong even if trust didn't collapse, non-uniqueness `[36]`).

**The cost to Chris, unchanged from `[20]`:** he still owes an account of *which* features count as P. The unicorn case gives one answer in his own vocabulary: P is whatever the conditional's antecedent names, and the antecedent is read from the [structure of the relata](../philosophy/morality/constitutive-middle-dialectics.md). That is O'Neill's move without Kant.

## Open Questions

- Is "no P-bearer ⟹ no moral fact" just the vacuous truth of the conditional restated, or a substantive claim? If it's only the former, the deontologist can concede it and lose nothing, and the real disagreement is level (a), grounding.
- Does lopsided error cost (§4) give a *rule* (precaution for possible sentience), and does that rule hold up against Pascal's-mugging cases (tiny probability, enormous claimed stakes)?
- Which level does the word "morality" name: rule form, or application? Most of the absolute-vs-relative fight may be a disagreement over that.
