## ⭐ 222 words per minute — and it is a fixed trait, not a choice

Chris: *"Charsky speaks rapidly in his opening, and it would be fun to count WPM here too."* Measured off the timestamped cues:

| | Words | Duration | **WPM** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Charsky opening, QF D** | 1,088 | 4.90 min | **222.0** |
| **Charsky opening, [round 2](./wordwar-r2-mass-deportation-charsky-ruelas.md)** | 1,083 | 4.87 min | **222.5** |
| Summerhays opening, QF D | 892 | 5.67 min | **157.4** |
| Charsky closing, QF D | 1,122 | 5.42 min | 207.1 |
| Summerhays closing, QF D | 878 | 4.97 min | 176.8 |

For calibration: ordinary conversation and broadcast delivery run **130–160 WPM**; audiobooks ~150; full competitive-policy *spreading* clears 300. **222 is the top of the intelligible range** — fast enough that a listener cannot pause on a claim, not so fast that words are lost.

**⭐ The striking number is not 222, it is 222.0 against 222.5.** Two different rounds, two different opponents, two different topics, and **his rate is identical to within half a word per minute across ~1,085 words each time.** That is not a tactical choice about a room; it is a **locked-in motor habit** from competitive policy debate. He does not modulate, and — on the evidence — probably cannot easily.

**What that does to [finding 6](./word-war-debate-series.md#cross-round-findings).** The finding says Charsky's delivery is a technique/format mismatch that costs him under an audience ballot. His three results at a constant 222 WPM are **+58.1, +58.6, −9.0.** **A constant cannot explain a variable.** So delivery is not what moves his outcomes — the prior is — and the honest version of finding 6 is narrower than it has been stated: *the mismatch imposes a roughly fixed cost, which a favourable prior swamps and a hostile one does not.* It is a handicap, not a verdict.

And then, unprompted:

**The negative declines to contest half of the resolution he was assigned.** Chris had called this before the tape: *"I can see why Summerhays took this debate — **he is a socialist, but he is also a nationalist, so he only wants to prove nations over no-nations.**"* Confirmed from the debater's own mouth, and he says it in his opening too:

> *"**My ideology is the only one in this conversation that places any importance on having a nation, on having borders at all.**"*

### ⭐ This answers the pre-tape question, by a mechanism the read did not anticipate

The [filed question](./word-war-debate-series.md#-qf-d--the-prompt-read-filed-before-the-tape-is-opened-2026-09-08) was whether the +9.0 means Charsky closed a near-unwinnable gap on execution, or whether the prompt's incoherence stopped the prior attaching. **It is the second — and the incoherence was exploited by the *Neg*, not merely suffered by the round.**

Because the two terms sit on different axes, Summerhays could **keep the popular half of his own label and discard the unpopular half of his opponent's**. He is not a capitalist arguing against socialism; he is a nationalist arguing against **cosmopolitanism**. So the pool's overwhelming anti-socialist prior — the one that should have made this the most lopsided round on the board — **was never mobilised, because nobody was defending capitalism.** What the audience actually voted on was *do moral obligations stop at the border?*, which for this pool is a far closer question.

**The prompt's defect is worth roughly the whole margin.** On the resolution as written, Charsky draws the worst hand in the bracket; on the debate that actually happened, he loses by nine. Chris's framing of the real proposition is exact: ***"can we feed the kid in Ghana, or is it just countries that stand on their own."***

This is failure mode **3** from the pre-tape read — *the round finds the real axis underneath and becomes a good debate about a proposition nobody assigned* — which was called, though the route to it was not.

## The Aff's opening is the most sophisticated in the bracket, and Chris's two objections both land

Charsky runs an anti-capitalism argument he credits to the philosopher **Nikhil Venkatesh**: capitalism blocks future-regarding reasons at three cumulative points.

1. **Registration** — *"what others need reaches producers as a price they pay… **future people cannot pay**, so nothing about them arrives through this channel,"* and the goods that matter most (a stable climate, non-extinction) are non-excludable and non-rival, so they resist internalisation even among the living.
2. **Weighing** — future considerations arrive discounted at the market interest rate, which prices *the risk that capital markets fail*, a risk that **necessarily exceeds the risk that society ends**. *"Markets can perish while people carry on."* So the discount rate tracks **the survival of an institution rather than of persons.**
3. **Action** — *"The CEO would rather not release the technology she believes is net catastrophic. But she knows that if she doesn't, someone else will… **Competition doesn't merely fail to transmit these reasons. It selects against anyone who acts on them.**"*

Point 2 is genuinely elegant and this page has seen nothing else like it in fourteen rounds. **Chris's objections go at 1 and 3, and both are right:**

- **On registration:** *"this argument doesn't seem to track, as **information also has a value that exists within the price**."* Correct — futures markets, long-dated bonds, insurance and catastrophe pricing all partially register future states, imperfectly and at a discount. Charsky's *"nothing about them arrives through this channel"* is stronger than he can defend, and the defensible version — *it arrives badly attenuated* — is the collapse into a market-failure argument he explicitly says he wants to avoid.
- **On action:** *"there is incentive for competitors to not release tech, and yet [he] goes and says competition forces them to anyway."* The deeper problem is that **this is a coordination failure, not a capitalism failure.** *If she doesn't, someone else will* is the structure of any decentralised system with multiple actors — including a world of independent socialist states, and including the borderless world Charsky wants. He has described a defect of **decentralisation** and charged it to **markets**.

## ⭐ The asymmetry that decides the philosophy — structural pessimism about markets, structural optimism about states

Chris's sharpest catch, and it is visible in one exchange. Perez puts the public-choice objection as well as it can be put:

> **Perez:** *"It seems like you're putting a lot of resources and power and decision-making up to a political class that has proven time and time again to be deceptive, disingenuous, and not have our interests at heart. **How will your theoretical good combat that historical reality?**"*
> **Charsky:** *"I think that there's just very plausible ways to sort of set up institutions that don't face these problems… **it's just sort of an empirical claim which sorts of mechanisms prevent them.**"*

> **Chris:** *"the Aff's answer is just that **we have to trust that we get it right**. He didn't show how capture happens or how to avoid it."*

**And the incoherence is with his own argument form, not merely with history.** His case against capitalism is that the defect is **structural and inescapable** — *competition selects against anyone who acts on the right reasons*, so appealing to ethical capitalists cannot help. **That exact argument transposes to political competition**: a politician who acts against her coalition's interest is replaced by one who doesn't; selection operates on office-holders as surely as on firms. Charsky's answer to it is that institutions can be designed around it — which is the *"ethical capitalist"* move he has already ruled out on his own side.

**So: no escape from selection pressure in markets, ready escape from it in states.** Nobody in the round named it, and it is the round's strongest available refutation.

### And the enforcement gap Chris predicted before watching

> **Chris, in advance:** *"the key observation will be the same socialism issue as always… **if he says this will be executed, by who?** There are no nations to enforce the 'moral' distribution… I would argue this contradiction of no borders, and yet we want to enforce socialist morality — how?"*

The prediction holds. Charsky's positive commitment is that entitlements track *facts about persons*, not *facts about their birth* — a genuinely borderless distributive principle. **Coercive redistribution requires a coercive agent**, and the only agents with that capacity are states, whose special standing he has spent the round dissolving. **The question is never asked**, which is why his *"the positive argument I gave for socialism went relatively untouched"* is true and also not to his credit.

## The Neg's definitional work — one canonical move and one loaded one

**The canonical one:** *"My definition of nationalism is that **the political and national unit should be congruent**."* That is **Ernest Gellner's** definition, verbatim, and using the standard academic formulation rather than inventing one is the strongest definitional move any contender has made in this bracket.

**⚠ Then he narrows it to America** — the nation is *"the propositional ideals that the country's founding fathers operated on… more accurately found in the Declaration of Independence."* Chris flagged the opening: **"the aff might be able to squeeze in here in that the topic is not US specific."** A resolution naming two general ideologies is answered with a *particular* nation's founding creed, which both smuggles in the most defensible version (propositional, not ethnic — letting him distinguish himself from white and Christian nationalism) and makes the claim untestable against every other nationalism on earth. Charsky never takes the opening.

**⭐ The loaded one, and it is the round's decisive definition:** *"My definition of socialism is **the workers controlling the means of production and populist globalism**. I'm going to argue that socialism necessarily includes globalism."*

He **bundles globalism into his opponent's term by definition** — textbook **loading** from [The Load-Bearing Word](./the-load-bearing-word.md): build the conclusion into the definition, and accepting the term concedes the resolution. Once it sticks, Summerhays only has to beat globalism, which is the fight he wants and the one he is equipped for. Charsky argues past it rather than refusing it, and by the interrogation round the bundling has done its work.

## ⭐ Left-coded premise, right-coded conclusion — second specimen

Charsky's cleanest challenge: *"why think that people are owed different things based on… **morally arbitrary differences in facts about their birth**?"*

Summerhays does not answer it as a rights question:

> *"It's not that they're owed, it's that **it's morally wrong to create a working class**… it's morally wrong to have a population that is robbed of their birthright and just has to work to send a never-ending stream of money overseas."*

**He answers a cosmopolitan-rights challenge with an exploitation argument** — globalism as a permanent transfer from domestic workers to capital and to foreign labour. That is a *socialist* premise driving a *nationalist* conclusion, and it inoculates him against the reply Charsky is holding.

**Second specimen of a move that keeps winning here.** [Rex Jones did the same thing in the amnesty round](./wordwar-r2-amnesty-jones-owlish.md) — amnesty as cheap-labour capture, a left-coded premise for a right-coded conclusion — and won by +65.2. **In a pool whose signature is anti-establishment before it is conservative, the right-coded conclusion travels furthest when it arrives on a left-coded premise.**

## Where the empirics went, and why they did not save the Aff

Charsky brings the round's evidence and Summerhays mostly declines to contest it:

- **Protectionism**: economists overwhelmingly against; even heterodox protectionism-friendly economists reject the version nationalists propose; infant-industry logic does not apply to a developed economy; better to climb the value chain than compete at the bottom of it.
- **H-1B**, and it is his best single argument: visas are allocated by **lottery**, which makes it a natural experiment — firms that win them **hire more domestic workers**, and industries with higher recipient rates show higher growth. *"H-1Bs act as an economic complement… they allow the company to get a really valuable resource for very cheap, which allows that company to grow, which means they will have to hire more domestic workers."*
- Summerhays' response: ***"I'm not going to criticize your stat because I don't know it."*** An honest concession, and expensive.

**But Summerhays pre-blocked the whole line in his opening, before it was run:**

> *"We are not concerned or obsessed with raising the GDP as liberals are, because **raising GDP just means the workers work more and the corpo[rations] sit more**. We're not concerned with cheap consumer goods all the time because we don't need it and it doesn't serve the nation."*

**He rejected the metric in advance.** So the evidence arrives and does not land — not because it is answered, but because the standard it is measured against was refused four minutes into the round. Chris: *"Luke counters this with a study that claims protectionism is bad — this is not necessarily true. **GDP go up isn't everything**… the trouble here is Luke is a globalist, so he won't accept the reasons for protectionism."*

Chris's own read on the H-1B mechanism is worth keeping as the qualification neither man supplied: *"there is some truth to this — getting a key worker at a cheap price can definitely help the company, but **the causation has to go that way**, and it starts breaking down if the conditions change (low-skill workers)."* The lottery result is real and its scope is firm-level and high-skill; it does not generalise to immigration as such, and Summerhays could have said so instead of conceding.

## The Hitler answer, and the counterexample nobody ran

> **Summerhays:** *"The reason why Hitler was bad was **not because of the ideology of national socialism**. It's because he had a race realist racial superiority ideology."*
> **Chris:** *"Don't like the answer that he accepts socialism! Hitler was only bad because he was a race realist! **Ack! What about Italy?**"*

The objection is decisive and free: **if you defend national socialism by isolating racism as the defect, you owe an account of the non-racial fascisms.** Mussolini's Italy is the obvious test — the racial laws arrived late (1938) and largely under German pressure, and the regime was corporatist, expansionist and repressive well before them. **Imperial Japan** is a second. Charsky asks the question, receives an answer that hands him the counterexample, and does not run it.

He also lets pass the follow-up answer that **national socialism need not be authoritarian** — *"you could have a democratic national socialist elected tomorrow."*

## Charsky flows the round in his closing — for the third time

His closing is a **flow**: *"the positive argument I gave for socialism went relatively untouched"*; *"the independent reasons to reject each of these commitments were sort of not really addressed"*; *"there wasn't really a response to either of the central arguments."* Chris: *"he goes through the flow and claims unargued points."*

**This is the third round in which he has done it**, and the vault flagged the cost the first time. The [formal-debate inventory](./formal-debate-concepts.md) lists **dropped arguments** as the one entry that explicitly **does not travel** to a lay ballot — *there is no judge flowing the round, so "he never answered this" reads as bookkeeping rather than as victory* — and the [deportation round](./wordwar-r2-mass-deportation-charsky-ruelas.md) recorded him closing exactly this way. He is doing the right thing for a format he is not in.

**Summerhays' closing goes the other way** and Chris grades it accordingly: *"a prewritten close that ties socialism to globalism and is basically a null."* He does not respond to the round; he restates the bundling that won it.

## ⭐ The Aff scored his opponent's concession as a win — the sharpest form of the flow problem

Chris: *"Here it was, I was expecting a socialism fight, and I get a nationalism fight where **the other side didn't realise what he was up against**."*

That is the round in one sentence, and it explains the closing. Charsky's summary rests on: *"the positive argument I gave for socialism **went relatively untouched**… the independent reasons to reject these commitments were **not really addressed**."*

**Both statements are true and neither is a point.** His socialism argument went untouched because **Summerhays agreed with it** — *"I do find myself agreeing with a lot of socialist things, that's why I'm not going to challenge Luke on the capitalism."* Under a flow, an argument the opponent does not answer is won. But an argument the opponent **concedes as irrelevant to what he is defending** is not unanswered — it is *off the table*, and the ballot has nothing to do with it.

**So Charsky spent his closing banking his opponent's agreement as if it were his opponent's silence.** That is a sharper failure than the [dropped-arguments](./formal-debate-concepts.md) problem already logged against him twice: it is not merely that a lay ballot cannot register unanswered points, it is that **the flow cannot distinguish *unanswered* from *conceded-and-therefore-moot***, and a debater reading his own flow will mistake the second for the first. Chris's verdict on the counterfactual is exactly right: *"**Luke easily wins if that was a policy debate round** for the reasons he said in his close, but ouch."*

## ⭐ Nobody scouts, and this round is what it costs

> **Chris:** *"I feel sorry for Luke not doing the research on his opponent well enough."*

Summerhays' position was **available in advance and decisive**: he is a nationalist who agrees with much of socialism, so the resolution was never going to be argued as written. Charsky arrives with a fully-built case against capitalism — which his opponent had no intention of defending — and no case at all against the borders proposition that actually decided the round.

**The bracket's evidence on opponent preparation is now two data points and they point the same way.** In [QF B](./wordwar-r3-iran-justified-tareyak-thunder.md), Tareyak's Lindsay Clancy analogy is the same case Thunder had used in his own previous round — possible scouting, unprovable from the tape. Here, the *absence* of scouting is unmistakable and expensive. **In a format where you can watch every one of your opponent's previous rounds for free, almost nobody does**, and the one round where it would have reframed the entire preparation is the round the better arguer lost.

## ⚠ On the question of what Summerhays personally believes

Chris: *"I almost want to do research on Summerhays to see if he is a neo-Nazi, as he just casually accepted national socialism… and it explains the reach if he is a popular influencer in that community."*

**This page will record what was said on tape and will not go looking for an affiliation.** The reasons are practical rather than delicate: the vault is a **public repository**, Summerhays is a named individual, and *"is he a neo-Nazi"* is precisely the class of claim that must not be published on inference from an hour of debate. The on-tape record is strong enough to stand alone and is already here — he said *"I don't reject that,"* he located Hitler's defect in race-realism rather than in the ideology, he declined to contest the anti-capitalism, and he allowed that the system need not be authoritarian. **Those are quotations, and they are the evidence.**

**And the analytically useful version of Chris's point does not require the accusation at all:** it does not matter what Summerhays privately holds, because **the position he stated on air was open to an attack Charsky never made.** The [Italy counterexample](#the-hitler-answer-and-the-counterexample-nobody-ran) defeats *"the ideology was fine, the racism was the defect"* regardless of who is saying it — Mussolini's corporatism was expansionist and repressive years before the 1938 racial laws, and Imperial Japan is a second case. That is a debate-craft finding, it is fully grounded in the transcript, and it costs nobody a reputation.

*(The reach half of the conjecture is also testable without any of this, and the [view data](./word-war-debate-series.md#-two-corrections-on-the-view-data-and-the-second-is-a-correction-of-a-correction) already speaks to it: Summerhays' +84.0 in round 2 is the largest unexplained mandate in the bracket, and nothing about its source is known.)*

## Who argued better

**Charsky, clearly, and Chris agrees:** *"Luke is the better debater and won the round, but Spencer has the bigger reach."* And Charsky himself gets the round's real shape right in his closing — *"they agree on socialism"* is, as Chris puts it, **spot on**.

**So the +9.0 is the bracket's cleanest specimen of the gap this hub exists to measure**: the better arguer lost, by nine points, in the round where the prior against him was largest on paper and smallest in fact. Two things had to happen for it to be that close — the Neg conceded the economic half, and Charsky is genuinely very good — and neither was enough.

⚠ Applying [finding 19](./word-war-debate-series.md#cross-round-findings): the strongest version of Summerhays' case is real and this page should say so. *Obligations do plausibly attach to thick relations rather than to abstract personhood; a polity that cannot prioritise its own members cannot sustain the solidarity that funds any redistribution at all; and a cosmopolitan distributive principle with no enforcing agent is not a policy.* He never assembled it in those terms, but the materials were his.

Two threads deliberately left open rather than resolved in a round review, both with the material already gathered here:

## Open Questions

- **Does the +84 kill the Iran double-header's registered calls?** It is the strongest evidence yet that this pool's war prior is **pro**-war, and both open quarterfinals were called for the anti-war Neg. Alternatively it is Summerhays' reach. **The two unread cards decide it.**
- **Is delivery a constant that can be discounted?** 222.0 and 222.5 across two rounds with opposite outcomes says yes for Charsky. Worth measuring for one more contender before the finding is rewritten.
- **What is the enforcing agent in a borderless distributive scheme?** The round never asked. It is the standing objection to Charsky's positive view and Chris flagged it in advance; worth a page of its own rather than a bullet.
- **Does the left-premise/right-conclusion move keep winning?** Two specimens, +65.2 and +9.0 — the second a *loss*, so the pattern is about **inoculation**, not victory. A third case would separate them.
- **Would refusing the "socialism includes globalism" bundle have changed the round?** It is the definition that decided what was argued, and Charsky argued past it instead of refusing it.
