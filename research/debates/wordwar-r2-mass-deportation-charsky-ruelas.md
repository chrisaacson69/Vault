## Argument Structures

### Aff — Charsky

1. **An explicit deductive frame, stated as such.** He runs a modus tollens over a chain of conditionals and *names the inference rule out loud* — if deportations are justified, the same balance of reasons would justify the same harm wherever that balance holds; denying the consequent yields the falsity of the antecedent.
2. **The dilemma he builds on it:** rejecting his second premise requires either that the state may deport non-violent *citizens* identical in every respect but status, or that **legal status alters the underlying moral facts** — i.e. that the moral law bends to state decree. He flags both horns as unintuitive and notes the pro side needs an account of **political authority** to get started.
3. **A quasi-experimental literature review** — named papers and identification strategies rather than aggregates: difference-in-differences on the staggered rollout of Secure Communities, quasi-experimental variation on firm outcomes, a calibrated general-equilibrium simulation. Claimed findings: enforcement reduced employment and wages for US-born workers (immigrants as economic complements), degraded firm performance, and produced **no statistically significant decrease in crime** despite large increases in detention and removal — plus reduced crime *reporting* among Hispanic victims and a corresponding rise in victimisation.
4. **The lump-of-labour diagnosis:** the pro-deportation case implicitly treats the number of jobs as fixed, so an added worker must displace an existing one.
5. **⚠ Two pre-emptive blocks, installed in the opening before any reply existed.** (a) A **standard-of-critique** demand — distinguish a *substantive* attack on an identification strategy from a generic gesture at confounding; name which identifying assumption fails and why. (b) A pre-emptive kill on the **home/country analogy**, called **question-begging**, since whether the state holds a property-like right over national territory is precisely what is in dispute.

### Neg — Ruelas

1. **Opens on having predicted the case rather than on answering it** — *"my opponent brought up exactly everything that I knew he would bring up."*
2. **Cost-of-living case:** a 2021–2024 entry surge into a housing shortfall; HUD-attributed rental-price growth; first-time-buyer age rising from 30 to 40; down payments above $40,000.
3. **Labour-market case:** immigrants as the bulk of prime-age workforce growth, the American-born workforce flat since 2019, most new jobs since early 2020 going to foreign-born workers — hence lower wages and weaker bargaining power.
4. **Fiscal arithmetic:** ~$68,000 lifetime net cost per illegal alien against ~$13,000 per deportation, therefore large net savings at scale.
5. **The electoral-motive case**, which he returns to and closes on: entrants were admitted to alter the electorate, amnesty has been abused, and removal yields *"more secure voting."*
6. **The home analogy, in the closing** — someone in your house without a lease *"is getting escorted out."*

### The critique of part 1 — why the deductive frame does not hold

Charsky's opening runs a modus tollens onto a dilemma: reject his premise and you must say either that the state may deport non-violent **citizens** identical in all respects but status, or that **legal status alters the fundamental moral facts**. He calls both horns wildly unintuitive.

**The dilemma is false, and the omitted option is the standard one.** There is a third position, and it is the one most defenders of territorial rights actually hold: **institutional facts do not alter fundamental moral facts, but they generate genuine obligations** — exactly as a promise or a contract does. Nobody thinks a signed lease rewrote the moral law; everybody thinks it creates real duties that did not exist before. Membership, consent and associative accounts of political obligation all live in that gap, and Charsky's two horns do not touch them.

Two further problems:

- **The stipulation does the work.** *"Identical in every way aside from citizenship status"* presumes status is morally inert — which is precisely the contested claim. The argument assumes what the dilemma is supposed to establish, which is the same charge he levels at the home analogy.
- **The political-authority demand is asymmetric.** He requires the pro side to supply an account of political authority before it can begin. But his own case needs one just as much: with no political authority there is no state duty to admit, protect or provide anything either. This is the [weaponized-Agrippa](../philosophy/tangents/the-negatives-easy-burden.md) move — running the regress on one side only — executed by the *affirmative*.

### ⭐ Chris supplied the rebuttal Charsky pre-registered as the only acceptable one — and Ruelas never found it

> **Chris:** *"This is a challenge even for a well versed economist like me. I would want to read all of his sources as I think they are making assumptions. Most immigrant/labor data is very limited and does not represent the large issues created by the 10M+ immigrants in recent times."*

Charsky's opening demanded exactly this and nothing less: not a gesture at confounding, but a named threat to identification. **This is that objection** — an **external-validity / extrapolation** challenge. Quasi-experimental designs on the staggered Secure Communities rollout identify the effect of *marginal* enforcement variation on *local* labour markets. They do not identify the general-equilibrium effect of removing millions, because that shock lies outside the range of variation the design exploits. A clean local estimate can be entirely correct and still say nothing about the policy on the table.

**And the evidence stack has a seam at exactly that point.** Charsky's mass-scale claim rests on the **calibrated general-equilibrium model**, which is not quasi-experimental at all — it is a simulation whose outputs follow from its calibration. So the rigour standard he polices ("name the identifying assumption") does not apply to the one citation that actually addresses *mass* deportation. Naming that seam is the substantive critique; **it was available all night and Ruelas never went near it.**

*(Stated as a structural reading of the case **as presented on the tape** — the papers themselves are unread here. Verifying them is the [project](#open-questions) Chris floated.)*

**Second round running, the decisive move was free and nobody took it.** In [suffrage](./wordwar-r2-womens-suffrage-tejeda-aftermath.md) it was *fiat*; here it is external validity.

### ⭐ The evidentiary standard was unmeetable in principle

> **Chris:** *"it was fair enough for a cursory look to show that these are all too myopic to reach conclusions. Marte tried to use more macro figures to expand the case, but **Charsky only asked for sources that didn't exist.**"*

**This is the strongest single observation about the round, and it inverts who was doing the right thing.**

Charsky's standard-of-critique demand — *name the identifying assumption that fails* — is unimpeachable as methodology and **unmeetable for this resolution**, because the study he is asking for cannot exist. There is no quasi-experimental estimate of removing ten million people, for the same reason the external-validity objection bites: **it has not happened**, so there is no variation to exploit. The evidentiary bar he polices therefore rules out the only class of evidence that could speak to the policy actually on the table.

Which means **Ruelas was reaching for the right *form* of evidence and failing on execution.** Macro aggregates — entry volumes, housing stock, fiscal totals — are the appropriate instrument for a question about a shock of unprecedented scale; they are what you use when the clean local estimate is out of sample. He simply could not source them, and lost the exchange on provenance rather than on relevance. The round's central methodological dispute is thus **the better-sourced side arguing at the wrong scale against the worse-sourced side arguing at the right one.**

### Why P1 fails — the level conflation, and three counterexamples

*Provenance: the P2 dilemma critique above was developed in this session. **The P1 analysis and the examples below come from a parallel session Chris ran on the same opening** and are recorded as that session's work. The two passes converged independently on the stipulation point — noted as convergence, **not** treated as verification, since the vault's standard is that independence means dropping to a lower artifact, not consulting a second agent.*

The premise: *if deportations are justified, the balance of reasons favouring them would justify the same course of harm in all other cases where the same balance of reasons is present.*

> **The parallel session's statement of the flaw, which Chris picked out as the best line:** *"P1's force comes entirely from the counterfactual — 'identical in every way aside from citizenship status.' But that phrasing stipulates that citizenship status is morally inert. Which is the thing under dispute."*

And the deeper diagnosis: **P1 conflates levels.** Universalizability is a constraint on *reasons* — like cases decided alike. It is not a denial that **institutional facts can be among the reasons.** If status is reason-giving at all, then two cases differing in status do **not** exhibit "the same balance of reasons," the antecedent is never satisfied, and the principle never reaches the case. The stipulation is what hides this: it smuggles in the denial that status is reason-giving, and the level conflation is what makes the smuggle invisible.

**Three counterexamples, from the parallel session** — each a case where a purely institutional difference generates real, uncontroversial differences in permission and duty, with nobody claiming the state rewrote morality:

- A man **identical to your spouse in every respect except that he is not married to you.** May the state compel you to share marital property with him?
- Someone **identical to a licensed surgeon except for the license.**
- A person **identical to a party to a contract except that they never signed it.**

The surgeon is the sharpest of the three, because it is not a matter of private consent between the parties — the license is a **state** instrument, and nobody concludes that stopping an unlicensed but otherwise identical practitioner means the state has bent the moral law.

### What survives of Charsky's case

The vault's [symmetric-grounding standard](./word-war-debate-series.md) applies to a verdict this harsh, so: two things stand.

- **The lump-of-labour diagnosis is simply correct.** Employment is not a fixed quantity to be divided; population changes shift demand, production, investment and hiring. Whatever else is wrong, that objection lands.
- **The crime-reporting mechanism is the most robust thing he cited**, precisely because it is *not* the claim that needs extrapolating. Enforcement reducing victims' willingness to report, and offending rising in consequence, is a within-scale behavioural finding. It survives the external-validity objection that guts the economic extrapolation.

The pattern is worth stating plainly: **his strongest evidence is the evidence that does not depend on scale, and his headline claim is the one that does.**
