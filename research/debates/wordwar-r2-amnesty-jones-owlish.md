## ⚠ The published prompt and the spoken prompt are different propositions

The video description frames a **pairing** — *"Amnesty vs. Mass Deportation,"* two options weighed against each other, burden shared. Perez states a **proposition** — *"we should give amnesty, not deport"* — and explicitly assigns Owlish the affirmative: *"because you are stating your position in the affirmative as a proponent of amnesty, you're going to go first."*

Those are not the same debate. Under the pairing, each side owes a comparative case. Under the proposition, **Owlish carries the burden and Jones needs only to deny**. She argues as though it were the pairing; he argues as though it were the proposition, and the format rewards him for it. **A new [defect](./word-war-debate-series.md#format-defects-and-their-cheap-fixes): the prompt as published and the prompt as read are not the same, and nobody reconciles them.**

## Argument Structures

### Aff — Owlish

1. **Frames the change as recent and politically driven:** immigration was uncontroversial until 2016; asylum entries fell 99.9% (attributed to a Cato Institute report) and immigration visas by half, so legal routes have closed and illegal entry is the only remaining recourse.
2. **The rootedness argument:** people already here have jobs, schooling, families and relationships — *"it seems unfair and inhumane to kick them out after they have already planted roots."*
3. **Selective-admission charge:** the administration extends asylum to some groups (Afrikaner farmers, El Chapo's family) who were not guaranteed jobs or income — *"we allow amnesty for those Trump deems acceptable. What makes the other people not eligible?"*
4. **ICE's cost and record:** $75bn appropriated, 170+ legal citizens detained, four named deaths, and deportation numbers barely matching Obama's.
5. **⚠ The opening closes on four demands rather than a case** — *defend the senseless deaths; answer why immigration has become more difficult; why does Trump admit whom he admits; what makes the others ineligible.*

### Neg — Jones

1. **Rule of law as the frame:** deportation is enforcement, not cruelty; amnesty signals *"that the law is optional and that those who break it most brazenly will be rewarded."*
2. **The queue-jumping fairness argument**, which he returns to more than any other: legal immigrants wait years, learn civics, take the test. This is the round's most-repeated move and his closing rests on it.
3. **Fiscal and wage strain** on healthcare, education and social services; downward pressure on the most vulnerable legal workers.
4. **Assimilation and cohesion** — without shared language, customs and values the result is *"social fragmentation."*
5. **⭐ The exploitation argument, and his most interesting move:** amnesty is driven not by humanitarian concern but by *"powerful economic interests that benefit from a cheap, vulnerable labor force"* — corporations, agricultural conglomerates, NGOs moving people through the Darién Gap. **A left-coded premise deployed for a right-coded conclusion**, and it inoculates him against the reading that he simply dislikes immigrants.
6. **A stated alternative to enforcement-by-force:** remove the ability to work, bank and rent — *"an economic non-violent solution."* He also says ICE has failed and volunteers that he is not a fan of it.

## ⚠ The strategic fact that decides the round: he refuses the partisan frame and she never adjusts

Owlish's case is built to prosecute **the Trump administration**. Jones declines the role:

> *"I don't have to defend Trump. I'm not pro-Trump. That's not my position… Trump has failed on mass deportations."*

He says versions of this repeatedly, and volunteers that Obama deported far more. **She keeps asking him to answer for Trump anyway** — *"why is Trump able to bring over whoever he wants?"* — and he keeps declining. So a large share of her prepared material lands on nobody.

This is the mirror of the [Biden/Trump round](./wordwar-r2-biden-trump-bourdeau-smith.md)'s lesson. There, a comparative resolution *forced* both partisans to run their standards twice. Here the resolution is **not** partisan, one debater imports the partisan frame anyway, and the other simply steps out of it — at which point the imported frame becomes dead weight. **Arguing against a position your opponent does not hold is expensive even when the criticisms are fair.**

## The reductio she accepted instead of blocking

The round's decisive exchange is short:

> **Jones:** *"Would you put any cap on the number that could come here at all? In India they have a billion people, China a billion people. Can 500 million Indians just come here and it still be America?"*
> **Owlish:** *"Yes, it would still be America. The country is still called America… I don't know what you mean. It's just the name."*

**The cap question is the standard test for whether an open-entry position has a limiting principle**, and the answer concedes there is none. Jones then uses it as the spine of his closing — he repeats it three times — because it lets him characterise her position as *America is just a name*, which is far more damaging than anything about immigration policy.

**The available blocks were cheap and she took none of them:** deny the hypothetical is relevant to the actual population under discussion (~20 million already present, which is her real proposal); accept a cap and argue about its level; or attack the frame — nobody's immigration position survives a hypothetical of 500 million arrivals, so the question tests nothing. Instead she affirmed the reductio.

**Under the [inventory](./formal-debate-concepts.md) this is a solvency/limits question**, and it is the one place where the Aff's failure to build a *specific* plan in her opening cost her directly: with no stated criteria, there was nothing to answer with.

*She does supply criteria later, under Perez's questioning — no serious crimes, work through the justice system, deport after conviction — but this arrives in the interrogation round, long after the damage.*

## Both closings characterise rather than argue

- **Jones' closing is almost entirely a recitation of her position** in his words, and much of it is uncharitable — *"she would say, haha, screw you"* to legal immigrants. But the parts that bite are the parts she actually said, which is what makes it effective.
- **Owlish's closing runs on the word *"ridiculous"*** — eight or more instances — and opens by claiming her opponent *"thinks that only Hispanic, white, black people, and Indians are meant to be in the United States."* That mischaracterises his stated position (he argued for *foundational* populations with US lineage, and explicitly said *"I didn't say white"*), and it is the kind of charge that is expensive if the audience heard the original.

**Neither closing summarises the dispute.** Compare the [Iran round](./wordwar-r2-iran-war-summerhays-cruz.md), where Chris noted both closings *"actually summarize positions well"* — this is the contrast case.

## The unsubstantiated-class claim, and an own goal

Owlish argues that people holding Jones' position *"are the same people who would go to McDonald's where somebody is working and point at them and say, why don't you get a real job."* Jones objects, notes he worked fast food himself, and asks her to substantiate it. She cannot, and answers *"you don't think that's something that happens?"*

**A claim about what a class of people would do, offered as an argument and then defended by rhetorical question, is the weakest form on the tape** — and it hands him a personal rebuttal he could not otherwise have earned.

## ⭐ Perez, round three — she changed her approach

Chris's standing question was whether she would. **She did, in two visible ways:**

1. **Her interrogation questions are genuinely clarifying and symmetrically uncomfortable this time** — she asks *Owlish* whether amnesty would be blanket or criteria-based (forcing the specificity her opening lacked), and asks *Jones* the classic libertarian right-to-work-and-travel question, pressing whether his objection is to entry or to citizenship. Each question is aimed at the weakest joint of its recipient's case.
2. **She explicitly stands down for the second open floor:** *"this is a war, a word war. So I'm going to back out and you guys are going to do 15 minutes of open debate."*

Across three rounds the arc runs **own agenda → own tangent → clarify and withdraw.** Whether that is learning or topic-dependence is unresolvable on n=3, but it is the right direction, and it is worth recording against the earlier charge.

*One handling note: the "personal attack" objection got a muddled ruling rather than a clean one, and the debaters resolved it themselves.*

## Open Questions

1. Does the **published/spoken prompt mismatch** recur across the bracket, or is this a one-off? It silently reallocates the burden of proof, which is the most consequential thing a format can get wrong.
2. Jones' exploitation argument is a **left-coded premise for a right-coded conclusion**. Is that a general technique worth naming — borrowing the opponent's coalition's frame to immunise a conclusion against its usual objection?
3. This is the **second comparative-shaped immigration round in round 2** (after [Charsky/Ruelas](./wordwar-r2-mass-deportation-charsky-ruelas.md)). Two immigration rounds in eight, and the bracket's most-watched tape. Is topic selection tracking audience appetite rather than spread?
