# Dad Saves America — Jevons, Say's Law, and Why Wages Are Made, Not Negotiated
> A 12-minute econ clip arguing that Jevons paradox is a surface symptom of **Say's law** (*demand is made possible by supply*): income is a function of output, technology raises output, and falling prices in one sector raise real wages in every other — so wages are **produced, not negotiated**, and AI is "just a new form of automation" riding the same spiral.

## Argument Structure (what was actually said)
1. **Cold open:** Hayek, *"If socialists understood economics, they wouldn't be socialists,"* against a clip of a socialist politician claiming the opposite (speaker unnamed in the transcript).
2. **Energy ↔ GDP is a two-way feedback loop.** Richer → bigger grid → richer. US home electrification 14% (1910) → 70% (1930). AI is the next energy-intensive turn of it.
3. **Jevons paradox** (1865, steam engines): efficiency raised coal consumption. Same thing now — better chips per joule → more AI use.
4. **Jevons the marginalist.** One of the three figures of the marginal revolution, which "pulled the rug out" from the **labor theory of value**: value is set at the margin by supply and demand, not by input cost — *"it doesn't matter how long it took to make this video, if you don't like it, it's not worth anything."* Claim: Marx was obscure until the Soviet Union promoted him.
5. **Jevons rests on Say's law.** *"Demand is made possible by supply"* — your income is a function of how much you can produce.
6. **Marxism is zero-sum and negotiation-only.** Its only route to higher living standards is bargaining power → unions. Wrong under technological change.
7. **The capital story.** Capital = stored labor (Smith); the farmer saves, buys the tractor/combine, produces 5–100×, and no longer needs his 10-year-old in the field. The weekend's economic cause is technology.
8. **⭐ The transmission mechanism.** Competition forces every farmer to adopt and cut price; food gets cheap; **the blacksmith's wage rises measured in time-to-buy-food** — even though nothing happened in smithing. *"That is how wages go up. Wages are the result of output… You can't negotiate when there's a fixed amount of income… you're just redistributing."*
9. **AI = automation.** Job-destruction-in-aggregate "has always been suspect." Host's own experience: building apps and doing research he couldn't previously afford or knew how to do — new supply, not substitution.
10. **Closing concessions.** Social/political disruption is a bigger threat than the tech; the **dignity of work** concern is legitimate and distinct from income; externalities, subsidies and corrupt political economy are real — "we live in a fallen world."

## Discussion

### Chris's main thread: supply dictates demand, the same claim from the other end
What Chris came for wasn't Jevons. It was the clip's Say's-law core:
> **Chris:** *"What I wanted to dig into was the idea that supply dictates demand! This is opposite, though basically the same, of what I was getting at with my equation of 'distributes to demand within the constraints of supply.' He is saying the same thing but from the reverse position… productivity creates goods, higher productivity means higher wages, and thus more demand. And this was the point: an increase in productivity is the way an economy grows."*

Chris's line ([The Supply Omission](../economics/the-supply-omission.md): *a market meets demand in the best available way **within the limited supply***) reads **from the demand side**. Demand is unbounded, so supply is the ceiling that binds it. The video reads **from the supply side**. Output *is* income, income *is* the purchasing power that turns wants into demand, so supply is what *creates* effective demand. These are the two ends of one loop, not two theories. Each fails when you delete the other end: demand-only analysis gives infinite output, and supply-only analysis would mean output nobody wants. (The video's marginalism point covers that second failure: *"if you don't like it, it's not worth anything."*)

**Chris's correction to the video, on population:**
> *"tbf, he is leaving out population growth… more labor × same productivity = more GDP too… but holding labor growth as steady, the only differentiation between economies is productivity."*

The decomposition is **output = labor input × productivity**. There are only two levers. Labor growth raises *total* output but not output per person. So once population is held fixed, productivity is the only thing that raises living standards. The video's "income is a function of how much you can produce" is true per head, not in total.

**Closing the circle, and Chris catching himself:**
> **Chris:** *"I think the reason I liked the video is it completed the circle of supply/demand. I got trapped by the same warning I have given before: you must look at it from both angles."*

**On AI:** it raises wages *because it raises productivity*, the same as the tractor. Chris: *"not that it causes people to disconnect."* The alienation cost (Seed 6) is real, but it is not the mechanism that sets wages.

### Seed 2: cross-sector transmission is why people fear productivity
The blacksmith example (#8) is the mechanism that connects the two ends of the loop. One sector's productivity becomes everyone else's real wage, and then new demand. Applied to the Jevons page's ATM/loom split, this is the missing half of the ledger: even when weavers lose, cloth buyers gain.
> **Chris:** *"I do think the point that cross-sector transmission is why people fear technology and increased productivity — the gains do not always land on those being affected."*

The aggregate case is right and it doesn't answer the fear, because the fear is about *who* gains, not *how much*. Growth sets the size of the pie, distribution decides who gets it (Seed 3), and technology changes are where the two most visibly come apart.

### Seed 1: source
This is not the unidentified explainer that started [Jevons for Software](../economics/jevons-software-demand.md). Chris: it *"can be used as such, the example is historical"*. The 1865 coal case is the same either way, so this clip can serve as the citable source for the paradox itself.

### Seed 3: negotiation is about carving the pie, growth is about its size
> **Chris:** *"Kinda. His point is that negotiating is arguing over a fixed pie, zero-sum, where he is trying to show what grows economies in total, not distribution. My point about negotiation is too myopic. It also deals with how to carve the pie after it is determined."*

So the two don't conflict, they answer different questions. The video is about **how big the pie gets** (productivity). The bargaining problem in [Value and Profit](../economics/value-and-profit.md) is about **how a given pie is divided**. "You can't negotiate your way to more" is true of the first and irrelevant to the second. Chris's correction is to his own seed, not to the video.

### Seed 4: energy ↔ GDP
Parked. *"We might dive into the energy = GDP relationship at some time, but it isn't what I was going after."*

### Seed 6: dignity of work, the one thing Marx got right
> **Chris:** *"Even he admits this is probably the only thing Marx got right: the division of labor makes people separate from what they do. An old-school farmer sees and knows the value they create; a receptionist, less so."*

This is Marx's **alienation** point, and it is separate from income. The same division of labor that drives productivity growth removes the worker's view of what they produce. So Say's law can explain rising wages and still leave this cost untouched. The cost is in **seeing the value you create**, not in having it.
