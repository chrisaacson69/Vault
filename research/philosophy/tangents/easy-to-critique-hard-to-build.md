# Easy to Critique, Hard to Build
> A venture's **costs are enumerable in advance; its benefits are not.** So any evaluation scored by listing consequences is biased against building — not because critics are wrong, but because the scoring rule is. The counter isn't to answer the list item by item; it's to refuse the rule and demand the counterfactual ledger.

## The asymmetry: one ledger is forecastable, the other isn't

Set two columns against any proposed venture.

**The cost column is legible in advance.** Harms are concrete, local, present-tense, and attach to identifiable people. You can name the displaced trade, the polluted river, the strained aquifer, the working conditions. They can be listed by someone who knows nothing about building the thing, and the list will be substantially *correct*.

**The benefit column is not.** The large returns are diffuse, systemic, arrive late, and are routinely **not the ones anyone projected.** They show up as second-order restructurings that had no name at the time of the argument.

This is not a claim that critics are wrong. It is a claim about **scoring**: a contest decided by *who can enumerate more consequences* is decided in advance, because one side is reading off the present and the other is guessing at the future. Enumeration is the wrong rule, and adopting it is the mistake — including for the builder, who is drawn into rebutting the list item by item and thereby concedes the rule.

> **Chris's statement of it:** *"It is easy to list the negatives of any venture, but the problem is this doesn't give any credit to the pioneers or the innate human ability to adapt to new problems... It is easy to critique, it is much harder to do."*

## The Model T case

The objection list available in 1908 was real and largely accurate: the horse economy and everyone in it — farriers, teamsters, stables, feed — displaced; urban pollution merely changed in kind; factory conditions; road deaths; noise. A committed critic could have written all of it, and been right about all of it.

**What was not on anyone's list** is what actually mattered. The **$5 day** (announced January 1914, roughly double the going rate, alongside a shortened shift) reset the terms of industrial employment far beyond one firm. And **cheap mobility restructured where people could live relative to where they worked**, which is a change to the shape of ordinary life that no 1908 ledger contained — because it isn't a property of cars, it's a property of what people do once they have them.

> **Chris:** *"It turned out that not only did the $5/day wage transform the nation, so did the mobility gained from cheap transportation."*

So the honest 1908 scorecard reads: **costs, correctly forecast; benefits, not forecast at all.** That is the general shape, not a quirk of this case.

## The second claim: adaptation is endogenous, and the venture funds it

The stronger half of the thesis is that the problems a venture creates **become the objects of the next round of invention** — and the venture itself supplies the pressure and the money for that round. A harm listed as a permanent cost is often a *temporary* cost with an inventor attached.

**The specimen arrived inside the debate that produced this page.** The Word War data-centers round spent much of its runtime on water consumption — and the thing being argued about, **closed-loop cooling**, exists precisely because water use became a binding constraint. The moderator's own live figures put evaporative systems at 1–3 million gallons a day against closed-loop-plus-dry-coolers below 10,000. The adaptation showed up *in the middle of the argument about whether the harm was tolerable*, and neither debater noticed that this was the point.

> **Chris:** *"Pushing our society to invent more and better water and energy resources is a bonus to this wave, not a hindrance."*

## ⚠ The scope condition — where adaptation is *not* reliable

**"Humans adapt" is true in aggregate and false in particular cases, and the particular cases are where people got hurt.** A page that stopped at the paragraph above would be Panglossian, and the counter-case comes from the *same industry and the same era* as the Model T.

**Tetraethyl lead.** The automobile's benefits were everything claimed for them, *and* leaded gasoline was a decades-long public-health harm that was flagged early, dismissed, and reversed only after sustained regulatory action. The adaptation did arrive — but it arrived through the **public-decision** mechanism, not through the pioneer's inventiveness or the market's. Asbestos and CFCs run the same way.

So the scope condition is precise, and it is the same one that governs [the residual-claimant principle](#the-underpinning-decision-rights-follow-the-residual) below:

> **Adaptation is reliable where the harm is priced to someone with both the incentive and the lever to fix it. It is unreliable where the harm is an unpriced externality landing on someone with neither.**

Water inside the fence is a cost to the operator, so closed-loop cooling gets invented. Aquifer drawdown under a **rule-of-capture** regime is a cost to the neighbouring well owner, who cannot price it and cannot fix it — so nothing gets invented, and the harm persists until a rule changes. *Same industry, same resource, opposite dynamics, and the difference is entirely who holds the residual.*

**Symmetry note, because it would be easy to cheat here.** The critic's list is *also* selected in hindsight. We remember the objection that looked silly (the horses) and forget the one that was right (the lead). Survivorship runs on both sides, which is exactly why the thesis has to be a claim about the **scoring rule** rather than about who tends to be correct.

## The underpinning: decision rights follow the residual

The reason the enumerator's standing is weak is not that his facts are bad. It is that **he is not the one who eats the loss.**

Whoever bears the residual — gains if it works, loses if it doesn't — holds both the sharper information and the standing to decide. A *"should"* addressed to anyone else is asking a room to overrule people with more skin in the game and more knowledge of the thing. This is why *"should Ford build more Model Ts?"* is not a policy question: it is a question **for Ford**, answered by whether they sell.

> **Chris:** *"It is mostly the businesses who are doing these things that are taking on all of the risk, and they are the ones that will get the benefits or suffer the losses for doing so."*

**The rhetorical move this page exists to name:** presenting a decision that already has an occupant as though the seat were **vacant**, so that an audience can be invited to sit in it. The tell is that the "should" has no agent and no mechanism by which the audience's answer reaches anyone.

And the scope condition bites here identically: the principle licenses the outcome **only where the decider bears the full residual.** Split the risk from the decision — an unpriced harm landing outside the firm — and the argument stops working, in the same place and for the same reason the adaptation argument stops working.

## Using it — the counter to a harm list

Answering the list item by item is the trap; it accepts enumeration as the scoring rule. Instead:

1. **Demand the counterfactual ledger.** Not *"what are this venture's harms?"* but *"what does the world without it look like — including its harms?"* The status quo is never costless and is never itemised.
2. **Test for non-uniqueness.** Does the objection apply to every large project? Then it is not an argument about this one. *(The Word War affirmative ran exactly this filter — "is this a reason not to build, or a reason to build differently?" — and it carried the round.)*
3. **Separate *stop* from *price*.** Nearly every item on a harm list argues for pricing, siting, or liability — not prohibition. Making the builder pay the cost is a different proposal from forbidding the build, and the two get smuggled together.
4. **Ask who adapts, and whether they hold a lever.** This is the scope condition, and it is where the critic's *good* arguments live. A harm inside the fence will be engineered away; a harm outside it will not. **Route the argument there and the debate becomes real.**
5. **Name the seat.** Who decides, and by what mechanism does this room's answer reach them? If neither can be answered, the "should" is decorative.

## Specimens

- **[Word War — Should America Build More Data Centers?](../../debates/wordwar-r2-data-centers-medeiros-horseradish.md)** *(primary, 2026-09-01)* — the pure form on both sides. The negative's case **is** an enumeration (farmland, aquifers, property values, jobs-per-acre, opacity, motive), delivered without a counterfactual ledger; the affirmative's non-uniqueness filter is the correct counter, imperfectly executed. Supplies the closed-loop-cooling specimen for endogenous adaptation and the Abilene aquifer specimen for the scope condition — **both, in one round.**
- **The Model T / $5 day** *(historical)* — the canonical case of a correctly-forecast cost column and an entirely unforecast benefit column.
- **Tetraethyl lead** *(historical, counter-specimen)* — the case that sets the scope condition: same industry, real harm, adaptation only via public decision.

## Open Questions

1. Is there a case where the **benefit** column was forecast accurately and the **cost** column was not? If the asymmetry is real it should be rare, and finding a clean counter-example would sharpen the thesis considerably.
2. Does the scope condition collapse into [externalities](../../economics/externalities-fact-check.md) with no remainder, or is "who holds the lever" doing work that "who bears the cost" does not?
3. The [Negative's Easy Burden](./the-negatives-easy-burden.md) is about *building vs. picking* in an exchange; this page is about *forecasting* costs vs. benefits. They pair, but do they share a root — or is the resemblance only structural?
