# The Public/Private Boundary
> Both sides of the socialism-definition fight draw the line on *ownership* — TIK by how many owners and how high the hierarchy, LiquidZulu by whether the owner is one person or a group. Both misclassify. The line that works is **access: who may be excluded, and by whom.**

## The two failed criteria

Both are attempts to say what makes an entity *public*, and both are stated as discoveries about the concept rather than as proposals.

**TIK — size and hierarchy.** *Public* = the hierarchy above the household; *private* = the individual or small family at its base. A company that "goes public" is therefore public: society at large can buy in. Alphabet becomes *"an internet public state."*

**LiquidZulu — collective vs individual.** The correct criterion is whether the owner is one person or many, because *"group ownership is a contradiction in terms."*

**They fail in opposite directions**, which is the useful part:

- LZ's rule makes every partnership, joint-stock company and joint bank account a **contradiction in terms** — it proves too much, and denies an ordinary institution rather than classifying it.
- TIK's rule makes **shareholder ownership** the mark of publicness, so an entity funded entirely by voluntary market revenue, excluding whomever it likes from its premises, counts as a state.

## Chris's criterion: who is excluded from control?

> **Chris (2026-09-23):** *"I don't buy TIK's idea that 'public' is about hierarchy and # of owners… **it is about access, not ownership.** Even in a family unit, something like a toy can be given to an individual and that is his private property — it does not have to be shared. And that is where the libertarian definition comes in: **who is excluded from control?** A public park, a public road, does not exclude who uses it. A corporate building does."*

**Private = there is a holder with a right to exclude. Public = no one may be excluded.**

This is not a new principle imported for the occasion. It is the vault's existing definition of property applied to a question it had not been pointed at: [self-ownership](./self-ownership-and-property-rights.md) is *"the right of exclusive control… the right to exclude others"*, and LZ's own ch. 6 runs the same line — *"a property right in X is the right to exclude others from X."* **So the public/private boundary and the property boundary turn out to be the same boundary**, which is a unification neither disputant gets from his own criterion.

### What it classifies correctly that the others do not

| Case | TIK (hierarchy) | LZ (collective) | Access |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A toy given to one child inside a family** | private (bottom of hierarchy) — for the wrong reason | ambiguous: a family is a group | **private** — the sibling can be excluded |
| **Public park, public road** | public | public | **public** — no one may be excluded |
| **A corporate building** | *public* (it's up the hierarchy) ✗ | *public* (many owners) ✗ | **private** — it excludes |
| **Alphabet / YouTube** | *"an internet public state"* ✗ | public ✗ | **private** |
| **NHS** | public | public | **public** — no one is excluded, and no one may opt out |

⭐ **The toy is the case that breaks TIK's rule from the inside.** Privateness does not require being small or low in a hierarchy. It requires an **exclusion boundary**, and one can exist *inside* the most collective unit there is.

⭐ **And TIK refutes himself in an aside.** Of Alphabet he says it is *"'private' in the sense that you or I can't just put our feet up on the CEO's desk, but it's still a 'public' entity because it is a public hierarchy."* Chris: ***"YouTube is not its own state! He even says one cannot put their feet on the CEO's desk!"*** On the access criterion that aside **is the test**, and it returns *private*. TIK notices the decisive fact and files it as a figure of speech.

## Why the disputants reach for ownership instead

Because the fight they are actually in is about **socialism**, which is defined in the literature as *social ownership of the means of production* — so both men inherit ownership as the axis. Note that TIK himself quotes the line that dissolves it, from Temin, and walks past it:

> ***"State control does not require ownership."***

If control is what matters and control is exercised by excluding, then ownership was always a proxy. The criterion that survives is the one about the exclusion right — who holds it, and against whom.

## Open Questions

1. ⚠ **The hard case: state facilities that exclude.** A prison, a military base, a restricted archive — these exclude aggressively and are unambiguously public. Does the criterion misclassify them? Two candidate repairs, unresolved: **(a)** the test is not access to *premises* but who may be excluded from *the entity itself* — nobody may opt out of the state that runs the prison, whereas anyone may decline to deal with Alphabet; **(b)** distinguish exclusion **by title** (an owner, private) from exclusion **by decree over property no one holds title to** (an authority, public). (b) looks closer, and it re-imports something like the consent/compulsion test as a second axis.
2. **Is *access* one axis or two?** Chris says both *"the state idea is more correct"* than TIK's hierarchy **and** *"it is about access."* If the political sense (state vs non-state) and the property sense (excludable vs not) are separate axes, a 2×2 would classify better than either alone — and would explain why one word doing both jobs produces the package deal in the first place.
3. **Does this survive the commons?** A public park is non-excluding *and* has an owner (the council) who can close it at night. Is it public because nobody is excluded in practice, or does the latent exclusion right make it private property the state happens to open?
