# Lazy Law — Banning the Tool Because the Wrong Is Hard to Reach
> Chris's term. When the real wrong is expensive to reach one case at a time, law regulates a proxy that is cheap to reach: the **tool**. The fix over-reaches, because every innocent use is caught. It also under-reaches, because the determined wrongdoer switches tools (*ban X, people do Y*). And the whole time, it looks like a solution.

**Links:** [Public/Private Boundary § the confiscation move](./public-private-boundary.md#the-confiscation-move--how-public-good-does-the-work) — where the substitution rule was first stated (Johnny's stick), [Mens Rea § The cross and the chair](./mens-rea.md#the-cross-and-the-chair-ban-the-token-and-the-meaning-moves), [The Free Will Principle](./mens-rea.md#the-free-will-principle), [The Slack in the Law](./slack-in-the-law.md), [Common Law as a Discovery Procedure](./common-law-as-discovery.md), [Registration and Scope Creep](./registration-and-scope-creep.md), [Scope Confusion](../scope-confusion.md), [Failure as Mandate](../../dynamics/failure-as-mandate.md), [No One May Dictate](../no-one-may-dictate.md)

**Origin:** Chris, 2026-09-29. It generalizes the cross-and-chair argument from the thread the [blind-pill review](../../../debates/blind-pill-ruelas-avg.md) started.

## The claim

> **Chris:** *"we get similar issue with guns and bullets and the like.. again, the law puts the onus on the tool and not the wielder.. The bigger concept is what I call **'lazy law'**. It is easier regulatorily to try to ban a tool instead of dealing with all of the specific instances of the tool use. We saw this too in the early days of the internet when companies went after the software companies and toolmakers who published file sharing tools instead of being concerned with those individuals who were breaking the law.. why? because it was easier. **A 'fix' that is broad and overcorrects because dealing with the specific cases is too hard.** I probably have many more examples, but we see this too often where laws tend to cut across generically instead of focusing on what is actually wrong."*

> **Chris:** *"This applies to the cross burning case too.. you got to the point that the crime is terrorism, but since this is hard to write into law, they go after the simpler things instead and think they are solving."*

## The mechanism

1. **The wrong lives in an act**, and acts are expensive to reach. Each one has to be detected, attributed to a person and proved.
2. **The tool is cheap to reach.** It can be defined, listed, registered and seized once, for everyone.
3. **So the law regulates the tool, and in doing so misidentifies the cause.** The [Free Will Principle](./mens-rea.md#the-free-will-principle) stops cause at the free agent who used the instrument. The law then pays twice:
   - **over-reach:** every innocent use of the tool is caught;
   - **under-reach:** the determined wrongdoer substitutes another tool.
4. **It looks like a solution.** A broad rule feels like doing something ([Scope Confusion](../scope-confusion.md)). When substitution defeats it, the failure becomes the argument for the next ban, *ban Y too* ([Failure as Mandate](../../dynamics/failure-as-mandate.md)).

**This is the Slack page's gradient, one step earlier.** [The Slack in the Law](./slack-in-the-law.md#-the-system-already-gravitates-to-the-cheap-measurements--this-is-a-diagnosis-not-a-forecast) records Chris's diagnosis that *enforcement* drifts toward whatever is cheapest to detect. Lazy law is the same drift at *drafting*: the rule gets written around whatever is cheapest to define.

## Specimens

| Specimen | The actual wrong | What gets regulated instead | The way around it |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Johnny's stick](./public-private-boundary.md#the-confiscation-move--how-public-good-does-the-work) | Johnny hurting people | The stick | *"he will find another"* |
| [Cross burning](./mens-rea.md#the-cross-and-the-chair-ban-the-token-and-the-meaning-moves) | Intimidation (Chris: *"the crime is terrorism"*) | The symbol | The chair, a coded sign |
| File sharing | Individual infringement | The software, and the people who publish it | Napster → decentralized P2P (Kazaa, Grokster) → BitTorrent |
| Guns and ammunition | Violence by the wielder | The firearm, the cartridge | Other weapons (but see the boundary below) |

**On file sharing, "why? because it was easier" is on the record.** The recording industry *did* try the individuals: it brought legal action against about 35,000 people between 2003 and 2008. It dropped the campaign after costly, embarrassing errors (children and dead people were among those contacted) and moved to working through ISPs. It chose the chokepoint because the specific cases were too hard.

**And the law has an anti-lazy-law doctrine on the books.** *Sony v. Universal* (1984, the Betamax case) held that selling a tool *"capable of substantial noninfringing uses"* is not contributory infringement. *MGM v. Grokster* (2005) narrowed that, but only by moving liability onto the maker's own conduct: distributing the tool *with the object of promoting infringement*. Even the narrowing aims at an act, not at the tool.

## Not every bright line is lazy law

The Slack page records Chris defending bright lines: *"people would rather take clear lines and deal with the 'injustices'"* ([rules and standards](./slack-in-the-law.md#rules-and-standards-and-the-trade-nobody-escapes)). That doesn't conflict with this page, because the difference is **what the rule is about**:

| | A bright line on the **act** | A bright line on the **tool** |
|---|---|---|
| Example | *no turn on red* | *no burning crosses*, *no file-sharing software* |
| Where its errors land | at the edges of the conduct (a rolling stop that happened to be safe) | built in: every innocent use, and every substitute |
| What it buys | predictability, at a known error rate | nothing for the public; the savings go to the regulator |

**The test:** does the rule name the wrong, or the instrument of the wrong? A rule on the act compresses the wrong. A rule on the tool swaps it for something easier to write down. A quick tell: restate an act rule as a tool rule and it turns absurd, so *no turn on red* becomes *ban cars that can turn right*.

## The strongest case against: where substitution is weakest (symmetric grounding)

- **Means restriction for impulsive acts.** When Britain replaced toxic coal gas with natural gas (1960s–70s), gas suicides disappeared and total suicides fell; the substitution was *incomplete* (Kreitman, 1976). Public health generalizes this: in a moment of crisis, friction saves lives. The gun version is Zimring's *instrumentality effect*: the same attack is deadlier with a gun than with a knife, so even a completed substitution does less harm.
- **Third-party enforcement can be efficient.** Law and economics defends enlisting gatekeepers (intermediaries such as ISPs, platforms and payment processors) when the primary wrongdoers are numerous, anonymous or unable to pay (Kraakman, 1986). That is lazy law, described approvingly.

**The boundary these suggest (not yet argued with Chris):**
- **Substitution is strongest against deliberate wrongdoing** by a determined agent. The terrorist, the hater and the pirate want the outcome and will find the next tool.
- **It is weakest against impulsive acts**, where a moment's friction changes the outcome.
- **Lazy law's usual targets are the deliberate kind**: hate, piracy, planned violence. Its best defense comes from the impulsive kind. Much of the fight over any particular tool ban is a fight over which kind of act drives its harm.

## The cure

**Reach the act, and pay for the aim.**
- **Cross burning:** prosecute the threat. That is token-neutral, and it is how *Virginia v. Black* told the rally from the lawn.
- **File sharing:** pursue the infringer, or the maker who actually induces infringement. *Grokster*'s test is about conduct, not the tool.
- **Violence:** prosecute the wielder.

Case-by-case aim is expensive, and [Common Law as a Discovery Procedure](./common-law-as-discovery.md) says what the expense buys. The information about what went wrong exists only in the individual case, and a broad statute is a central plan that skips the discovery.

## Open Questions

1. **The impulsive-act boundary.** Does Chris accept means restriction for impulsive self-harm (coal gas, bridge barriers) as a genuine exception? If so, what separates a tool ban aimed at impulsive acts from lazy law? The case against gun regulation has to meet this, because it is where the other side is strongest.
2. **Gatekeepers.** Is enlisting an intermediary always lazy law, or is it legitimate where the intermediary can actually observe the act? *Grokster*'s conduct test suggests one line.
3. **More specimens.** Chris: *"I probably have many more examples."* Candidates to test against the act/tool test:
   - DMCA §1201's ban on circumvention tools, which applies even to non-infringing uses;
   - pseudoephedrine purchase limits aimed at home meth labs;
   - weapon definitions drawn by feature rather than by use.
