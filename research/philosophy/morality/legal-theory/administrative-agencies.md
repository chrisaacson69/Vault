# Administrative Agencies and the Power to Execute
> Congress legislates; the executive executes. Agencies are executive *staff*, so on a unitary-executive reading they should answer to the elected President — which is where the law has now moved (*Loper Bright* stripped their power to "say what the law is," 2024; *Trump v. Slaughter* overruled *Humphrey's Executor* and restored at-will removal, 2026). The live puzzle Chris names: the President has robust power to *decline to enforce* a law (DACA) yet, until 2026, could **not** fire the staff executing it — backwards, if agencies are just the executive's hands. The deeper fork is *who fills the gaps* Congress leaves: an insulated expert agency (illegitimate — unaccountable) or the elected executor (legitimate — removable). The whole fight is **who holds the unavoidable discretion**, and whether that holder is accountable.

## The constitutional problem — the "fourth branch"

The Constitution vests **all** legislative power in Congress (Art. I §1), **the** executive power in the
President (Art. II §1), and charges him to "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed" (Art. II §3).
There are three branches, and the enumeration is a *limiting* one (see
[Originalism and the Limiting Document](../../../originalism-and-the-limiting-document.md)). An
administrative agency sits athwart that scheme: it **makes rules** (looks legislative), **adjudicates
violations** (looks judicial), and **enforces** them (executive) — often all three, inside one body, staffed
by unelected experts partly insulated from the President. This is the "fourth branch" the text never
authorizes, and it is the structure the [16th + 17th Amendments made politically possible](./represented-unit.md):
unlimited revenue plus senators unmoored from state control. This page is the downstream chapter of that
story.

## Chris's position — a unitary-executive reading

Stated and developed with Chris; the through-line is that **agencies are executive staff, not a branch**:

1. **Agencies shouldn't make law.** "It makes no sense these agencies can make law; the Constitution has no
   provision for such a thing." *Loper Bright* (below) is "a big win."
2. **Humphrey's Executor makes little sense; Seila was right to revisit** — and Chris's view is now the law
   (*Slaughter*, 2026, finished the job).
3. **Congress legislates, the executive administers**, so every agency "falls under the privy of the
   executive to execute." The President is the accountable head of the whole executive branch.
4. **The asymmetry is backwards** (see below): a President may issue an EO to *not enforce* part of a law
   (DACA) but — pre-2026 — could not *hire and fire* the agency at will. More control over *whether to
   execute* than over *who executes*.
5. **Congress's vagueness passes discretion to the executor** — *"if Congress is too lazy to make things
   explicit enough, then the executor gets to fill in the gaps"* (the accountable President, not an insulated
   agency). But on reflection this is Chris's *fallback, not his preference*: the fix should be to **stop the
   vagueness** (nondelegation), because normalizing executive gap-filling just lets each administration
   legislate via the executive — see his resolution below.
6. **Preferred end-state:** "the executive is the one who determines how to execute clear law, and not hand
   everything over to an agency." Some administrative staff is unavoidable — administration must be done —
   but it is *staff under the executive*, not an autonomous rule-making state.

## The four grounded cases

| Case | Year | Holding | Bears on Chris's point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Humphrey's Executor v. United States** | 1935 | Congress *may* limit removal of FTC commissioners to "inefficiency, neglect, malfeasance"; they're "quasi-legislative/quasi-judicial," not purely executive. | The precedent that built agency *independence* — the one Chris says "makes little sense." |
| **Seila Law v. CFPB** | 2020 | For a **single-director** agency wielding **executive** power, for-cause removal is unconstitutional (5-4, Roberts). | The first cabining of Humphrey's — "Seila took another look." |
| **Loper Bright v. Raimondo** | 2024 | **Overruled *Chevron*.** Courts exercise *independent judgment* on what a statute means (APA §706); no deference to an agency just because the statute is ambiguous (6-3, Roberts). | "A big win" — strips agencies of the power to "say what the law is." |
| **Trump v. Slaughter** | 2026 | **Overruled *Humphrey's Executor.*** Congress cannot restrict at-will removal of FTC commissioners; "Humphrey's framework… has not withstood the test of time" (6-3, Roberts). | Chris's position becomes the holding — agencies answer to the President. |

**The Fed wrinkle (*Trump v. Cook*, 2026, companion).** The same Court **carved out the Federal Reserve**
(5-4, Roberts): central-bank independence is a "distinct historical tradition," and any change "must come
from Congress, not the courts." A pure unitary-executive theory has no room for this exception — it's either
a principled *sui generis* carve-out or an unprincipled one that proves the rule. (Open question below.)

## The asymmetry Chris flagged — is "backwards" right?

The puzzle: a President wields **robust** power to *decline enforcement* (DACA) but had **restricted** power
over *personnel* (removal). Enforcement discretion is deep-rooted — *Heckler v. Chaney* (1985) makes agency
**non-enforcement presumptively unreviewable**, on the logic that the executive can't pursue every violation
and must set priorities; choosing them *is* executing. So the President could effectively nullify a statute's
application while being told he couldn't fire the officials applying it. From a unitary-executive standpoint
that **is** backwards: the President's most core power (directing his own subordinates) was weaker than a
more peripheral one (enforcement triage). **The 2026 correction (*Slaughter*) resolves the asymmetry in
exactly Chris's direction** — both powers now robust.

**But the DACA half deserves a sharper cut than "he ignored the law."** There are two different acts inside
DACA, and only one is clean:

- **Non-enforcement** (declining to deport a defined class) — squarely executive, *Chaney*-protected. Not
  controversial as a matter of power.
- **Affirmative conferral** (granting work authorization, a lawful-presence posture, downstream benefits) —
  this looks like *making* policy with the force of law, and it's why DACA was litigated. Tellingly,
  *DHS v. Regents* (2020) struck the *rescission* as arbitrary-and-capricious under the APA **without ever
  ruling on whether DACA itself was lawful** — the Court dodged the merits. So DACA is *not* a pure "President
  ignores a law" specimen; the contested part was the affirmative program, i.e. the executive doing something
  that resembled legislating. The asymmetry is real, but the DACA example is doing double duty — it mixes
  clean non-enforcement with contested benefit-creation.

## The tension inside Chris's own position — and its resolution

Two of Chris's commitments pull against each other, and naming the pull is where the position gets sharp
(this is arguments-over-rhetoric applied to *his* view, not a gotcha):

- **"Agencies shouldn't make law"** (Loper Bright), **and**
- **"If Congress is vague, the executor fills the gaps."**

But **gap-filling *is* a species of lawmaking.** If a statute says "set safe emission levels" and the
executive picks the number, that number carries the force of law — the very thing *Loper Bright* and the
nondelegation worry are about. So the two commitments can't both be about *whether* quasi-legislative
discretion exists. The reconciliation — which Chris's own words point to — is **accountability, not
absence:**

> The discretion Congress leaves is unavoidable. The question is never *whether* someone fills the gap, but
> **who** — and whether that someone is *removable by the voters.* Gap-filling by the **elected, at-will-
> removable President** is legitimate *execution* (one throat to choke); gap-filling by an **insulated expert
> agency** (the old *Chevron* regime) is illegitimate *because unaccountable.*

That is the **exact same "who holds the discretion" structure** as the [corruption fork](./corruption.md)
(whose purpose fixes the office?) and the [represented-unit argument](./represented-unit.md) (who does the
Senator answer to?). The vault keeps landing on it: **the design question is almost never "should this power
exist" but "who holds it, and can they be fired."**

## The nondelegation fork — two cures for a lazy Congress

Chris's "if Congress is too lazy, the executor fills the gaps" is one of **two** constitutional responses to
vague statutes, and they conflict:

- **(a) Force Congress to be specific — nondelegation revival.** Under the lenient "intelligible principle"
  test (*J.W. Hampton* 1928; *Whitman v. American Trucking* 2001) Congress may delegate broadly. A revival
  (signaled in the *Gundy* 2019 Gorsuch dissent) would **invalidate the vague delegation itself** — there'd
  be no gap to fill, because Congress would have to make the call. This is the reading most faithful to "*all*
  legislative Powers… vested in *Congress*."
- **(b) Let the executor fill the gap** (Chris's lean). Given the gap, the accountable President fills it.

They diverge on the root: (a) says the gap **shouldn't exist**; (b) says **given** it exists, keep the filler
accountable. (b) is more workable (Congress will always be somewhat vague) but concedes that
quasi-legislative discretion is permanent and merely *relocates* it to the executive — which is fine on
accountability grounds but does **not** fully answer the Article I objection that *someone other than
Congress is making binding rules.*

**Chris's resolution — (a), make Congress do the work.** The executive-gap-filling route is itself an
[innocuous-thread pull](./registration-and-scope-creep.md): normalize it and *"each administration would
basically legislate via agency"* — the exact thing *Loper Bright* was meant to stop, re-entering through the
executive's door instead of the agency's. He doesn't *like* executive gap-filling either; he only
acknowledges the President has *some* discretion over the *manner* of execution (the irreducible residue).
The primary cure should be forcing Congress to be explicit — put the accountability where the Constitution
already put the power. So the stack is: **nondelegation to shrink the gaps first; unitary-executive
accountability only for the irreducible remainder.**

## Connection to corruption — insulation is the capture invitation

The removal fight is the [corruption](./corruption.md) question one level down. An agency insulated from the
elected executive answers to… whom? On the unitary view, a nominally-neutral but unremovable body drifts to
serving **its own institutional purpose** — Olson-style capture, the exact "unaccountable actor serving a
private/institutional end behind a public charter" the corruption page identifies as the *masked* failure
mode. *Humphrey's* "independence," from this angle, wasn't neutrality; it was an **invitation to capture with
no removal remedy.** Restoring removal (*Slaughter*) is a **re-accountability** move: it doesn't guarantee
good agencies, it guarantees a *fireable* one — feedback where there was insulation (cf. the friction-vs-
feedback distinction in [Gödel governance](../../dynamics/the-godel-governance-problem.md)).

## Chris's positions on the hard questions

- **The Fed carve-out is illegitimate ("BOO").** Chris rejects *Cook*: *"I don't like how 'traditionalism'
  makes law — we have the text to answer that."* A [textualist/originalist](../../../originalism-and-the-limiting-document.md)
  has no room for a *sui generis* central-bank exception invented from "distinct historical tradition"; the
  vesting clauses don't carve out monetary policy, so neither should the Court. He reads *Cook* as **SCOTUS
  dodging the bigger question** it had just answered everywhere else — deciding the FTC cleanly then flinching
  at the Fed. (Note the tension the Court created for itself: it rejected the independence rationale in
  *Slaughter* and then honored it in *Cook* twelve hours later. Chris's objection is that "tradition" is
  doing the work the text should do.)
- **Accountability's relocation risk is real — which is *why* the cure should be Congress, not the President.**
  Chris grants the worry: presidential control *"could turn into unraveling as each new president reshapes the
  landscape every time."* An executive-centered fix trades stable-but-unaccountable agencies for
  accountable-but-whipsawing ones. His conclusion follows directly: **make Congress the accountable one.**
  Durable rules set by the legislature (hard to pass, hard to undo — [friction](../../dynamics/the-godel-governance-problem.md))
  beat rules that flip every four years with the White House. This is the same reason he picks nondelegation
  (a) over executive gap-filling (b) above: put both the *power* and the *accountability* on Congress, where
  the text already assigns them.

## Will Congress do the work? — Chris's diagnosis (pessimistic, and structural)

Chris's honest answer to the question below: *"I wish I had the answer… my hopes are not high."* But the
pessimism is **structural, not a mood** — he names the exact failure paths, and they're the deepest point of
the session because they explain why the accountability cure is so hard to reach.

**The proposed forcing function:** let the President *"rule via margins until Congress realizes it won't stop
without them"* — i.e. make executive overreach salient enough that the legislature reclaims its own power in
self-defense. This is the Madisonian bet: [Federalist 51](../../../originalism-and-the-limiting-document.md)
assumed *"the interest of the man must be connected with the constitutional rights of the place"* — that an
officeholder identifies with his **branch** and will defend its prerogatives, so *ambition counteracts
ambition.*

**Why the bet fails — the loyalty the design assumed was swapped out.** Two failure paths, both fatal:

1. **Co-partisan capture of the check.** *"Often a majority in Congress approves of the president, so they
   don't mind it happening or even encourage it."* Madison's mechanism assumed *institutional* loyalty; real
   officeholders have *partisan* loyalty. A congressman of the President's party won't defend congressional
   power against his own team — so "ambition counteracting ambition" becomes ambition *aligning* with
   ambition. (This is the *"separation of parties, not powers"* problem: the operative cleavage is party, not
   branch, so the branch-based check idles whenever one party holds both.)
2. **The out-party routes around instead of repairing.** *"Those that do not like it will look for alternate
   legal paths such as SCOTUS rulings to get their way without fixing the problem."* The losers don't reclaim
   *legislative* power (the front-door fix); they seek a *judicial* win — the
   [enforcement-bypass](../../dynamics/the-godel-governance-problem.md) again — which resolves the instant
   grievance while **entrenching** the bypass pattern for next time. Winning the case beats fixing the
   structure, every time, for whoever is currently losing.

**The result is incentive-incompatible self-repair** — the [Gödel-governance](../../dynamics/the-godel-governance-problem.md)
core in one sentence: *the structural fix requires every actor to forgo the advantage the broken structure
hands them whenever they're winning,* so no one with the power to repair it has the incentive to. Restoring
the original accountability requires getting the structure *back to where it was*, but each player prefers to
exploit the current break while they're ahead. Chris's read on the recent wins (*Loper Bright*, *Slaughter*):
*"glad to see some people coming to their senses… but there is still more to go"* — the courts have removed
one bypass (agency lawmaking) and restored one control (removal), but the deeper repair (Congress reclaiming
the legislative function it delegated away) runs straight into the incentive wall above, and courts can't
force that from outside.

## Open Questions (still live)

- **What minimal administrative staff is unavoidable**, and how do you keep "staff that executes" from
  ratcheting back into "agency that legislates" (the [scope-creep](./registration-and-scope-creep.md)
  problem applied to the executive's own machinery)? Chris's end-state — *the executive determines how to
  execute clear law, minimal handoff* — needs a boundary that itself resists creep.
- **Will Congress actually do the work?** The nondelegation cure assumes a legislature *willing* to be
  explicit. The whole reason broad delegation arose is that being specific is politically costly (you own the
  hard call). If nondelegation forces specificity and Congress simply *won't*, the result may be paralysis
  rather than accountability — is that an acceptable price, or does it just re-route the pressure back to the
  executive anyway?
