# Common Law as a Discovery Procedure
> Legislation is central planning for conduct and meets the calculation objection head-on. Adjudication doesn't demand the information in advance — it generates it at the dispute, from the parties who hold it. Statute plans; precedent discovers.

**Links:** [The Slack in the Law](./slack-in-the-law.md) (**the parent specimen** — the calculation argument arrived there), [Aggregation vs. Sorting](./aggregation-vs-sorting.md) (*voting aggregates, markets sort* — adjudication belongs in the sorting column), [Mens Rea in Libertarian Law](./mens-rea.md), [Government Formation](./government-formation.md), [The Gödel Governance Problem](../../dynamics/the-godel-governance-problem.md) (*we need what we cannot complete*), [Registration and Scope Creep](./registration-and-scope-creep.md)

## Where this came from

Chris, working through why no legal code can decide every case:

> *"Action is **infinite**, laws are **finite**… it felt the same as the math showing a simulation of reality is greater energy than just reality, and that a computer **cannot solve the ECC** as it would take more information to recreate it than just live it."*

Then, on the consequence: *"I like the **common law / market** connection as this comes up all the time and **deserves its own look** as it connects to so much."* This page is that look.

## The argument

**A legal code is a central plan for conduct, and it faces the Mises–Hayek calculation objection in its original form.** The information a planner would need to price every good is *dispersed across the people who hold it, largely tacit, and continuously generated by the very activity being planned* — so it cannot be assembled in advance. **Substitute *"decide every case"* for *"price every good"* and the argument goes through unchanged.**

**Adjudication is the institutional answer with the same shape as a market.** It does not demand the information ex ante. It waits for a dispute, at which point:

- the information becomes **locally available** — the parties were there, and they know what happened;
- it is **elicited under adversarial pressure**, which is a mechanism for surfacing what each side would rather omit;
- the decision is made by people **embedded in the local context** (jury) rather than by a drafter imagining the case years earlier;
- and the outcome is **published as precedent**, which is the aggregation step — the accumulation of many local decisions into a signal that later parties can read.

**Precedent is to case knowledge what price is to production knowledge:** a compressed public summary of a great many private judgements, carrying more information than any participant possesses and none of them had to state.

## Hayek made this argument, and named the two things

This is not an analogy invented here. **Hayek's *Law, Legislation and Liberty* distinguishes two kinds of law**, and the distinction is the spine of this page:

| | ***nomos*** — grown law | ***thesis*** — made law |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | discovered through adjudication of disputes | enacted by a legislature |
| Aims at | resolving conflicts between private parties | achieving a specified outcome |
| Knowledge required | **local, supplied by the parties** | **global, must be held by the drafter in advance** |
| Analogue | the price system — a **spontaneous order** | the central plan |
| Failure mode | slow, path-dependent, can entrench error | over-reach, unintended consequences, the calculation problem |

His claim is that a legislature writing general rules of conduct is doing something **categorically different** from a court resolving a dispute, and that conflating them is how the second kind quietly displaced the first.

## ⚠ Where the analogy strains — four places, stated up front

A page that only made the case would be advocacy. The disanalogies are real and some are serious:

1. **Feedback is sparse and slow.** Prices update continuously and cheaply; a precedent requires someone to litigate, at expense, and may take years. **A market with one price update per decade would not clear either.**
2. **Precedent locks in.** A wrong price is corrected by the next trade; a wrong precedent is *load-bearing for everything decided after it*, and **stare decisis is a mechanism for preserving error**, not just knowledge. The path-dependence that makes common law stable also makes it slow to repair.
3. **Judges are not price-takers.** A market participant cannot set the price; a judge sets the rule. **Adjudication concentrates decision authority in a way exchange does not**, which reopens every capture and selection worry — and connects directly to [the Gödel Governance Problem](../../dynamics/the-godel-governance-problem.md): who constrains the constrainer?
4. **⭐ Law needs something markets do not: ex-ante knowability.** You want to know the price *when you trade*; you want to know the rule *before you act*. **Predictability is an argument for codification**, and it is the reason [bright-line rules are chosen deliberately](./slack-in-the-law.md#-the-threshold-problem--and-why-bright-lines-are-chosen-not-merely-inherited) despite their known error at the margin. This is the strongest objection on the list, because it is not a defect in the discovery mechanism — it is a **genuine competing value the mechanism cannot supply.**

**Which is probably why every mature system runs both** — statute layered over common law — rather than choosing. Not confusion: a division of labour between *predictability where it matters most* and *case-fit where it matters most*.

## ⭐ What this does to the automation problem

The [parent page](./slack-in-the-law.md) argues that automated enforcement pushes law toward **rules and away from standards**, because a machine can apply the first and not the second. Read through the frame here, that is a sharper claim than it first appears:

> **Automated enforcement is a mechanism for converting *nomos* into *thesis*.**

It cannot enforce a standard, so standards become dead letters; it can enforce a bright line perfectly, so bright lines become the operative law. **The discovery procedure is not repealed — it is starved**, because disputes stop reaching adjudication once the machine has already disposed of them. No precedent forms, no local knowledge is elicited, no signal accumulates.

**A society that automates enforcement therefore stops generating the information its own legal order runs on**, which is the calculation problem arriving by the back door: not a planner who cannot get the knowledge, but a planner who **destroys the process that was producing it**.

## Open questions

- **How much of this survives the fact that modern "common law" systems are overwhelmingly statutory?** If *nomos* is already a thin layer on a thick *thesis*, the discovery mechanism may be describing a system that no longer exists. **This is the first thing to check, and it may deflate the whole page.**
- **Does adversarial elicitation actually surface truth, or wealth?** The market analogy assumes the process reveals what the parties know. Litigation cost, representation quality and settlement pressure all argue it partly reveals **who could afford to keep going** — the same objection as *markets allocate to willingness-to-pay, not to need*.
- **Is precedent really an aggregation mechanism, or just a lock?** Prices aggregate *and* clear; precedent aggregates but never clears. What would the clearing mechanism be — legislative override, overruling, desuetude?
- **Does the strain-list have a systematic shape?** Three of the four disanalogies (sparse feedback, lock-in, judicial authority) are about **the absence of a cheap correction mechanism**. If that is the common root, the interesting design question is not *"statute or precedent?"* but *"what is the legal analogue of a liquid market?"*
- **Connection to [Aggregation vs. Sorting](./aggregation-vs-sorting.md), unworked.** That page holds that voting aggregates and markets sort, and that increasing returns to coordination make law a **network good** which consolidates even without coercion. **If law is a network good, is a distributed discovery procedure stable at all** — or does it consolidate into a single legislature by the same mechanism? That tension is not resolved here and may be the most important open item.
- **Is there a third form?** *Nomos* and *thesis* may not exhaust it — arbitration, standards bodies, and insurance underwriting all produce binding conduct rules by neither route. Worth a look, since they are the closest live examples of law-by-discovery in a modern economy.
