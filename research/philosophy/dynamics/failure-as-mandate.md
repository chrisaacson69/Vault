# Failure as Mandate
> Any agent whose existence is justified by a problem is paid to keep the problem, and, when the loop is inverted, *rewarded* for failing to fix it. Failure doesn't cost the failer authority; it becomes the argument for giving them more. Not only a government pathology.

**Links:** [Plato's Ship of State (specimen that spawned this)](../../debates/plato-ship-of-state-charisma-ratchet.md), [History Repeats — Small-Pain-Tolerance](../../../notes/history-repeats-small-pain-tolerance.md), [Government Formation (the exit-cost ratchet)](../morality/legal-theory/government-formation.md), [Fiscal Pressure Must Leak Somehow](../../../notes/fiscal-pressure-must-leak-somehow.md), [Business Cycles](../../economics/business-cycles.md), [The Gödel Governance Problem](./the-godel-governance-problem.md), [Scope Confusion](../morality/scope-confusion.md)

## The thesis (Chris, 2026-09-24)

> *"If the problem gets solved, there is no longer a reason for them to exist anymore. Plenty of incentives to create problems, plenty of disincentives to fix those problems."*

Three escalating forms:
1. **Preservation:** don't solve the problem you exist to solve. (Treatments over cures.)
2. **Creation:** manufacture or inflate problems only you can fix. Chris: *"politicians love to point out or even create problems just to convince others they are the only ones to fix them."*
3. **Inversion:** your own failure becomes the mandate. *"Govt makes problem, now we need more govt to fix that problem!"* This is the ratchet form, because each click enlarges the agent that clicks it.

## Where it sits in the ratchet family

The [small-pain note](../../../notes/history-repeats-small-pain-tolerance.md) says the feedback loop *never closes* on the actor: the consequence lands after they're gone. Failure-as-mandate is the stronger claim, that the loop **closes inverted**. It is the misattribution link in the delegation loop from the Ship of State discussion: delegation → lag → **misattribution** → consolidation → complexity → delegation.

## The tension to resolve: inefficiency vs ratchet

Chris, earlier ([therapy debate](../../debates/wordwar-western-therapy-men-summerhays-gilkison.md)): a perverse incentive to extend treatment is real, *"but I don't think this is good enough to say it is not 'working.'"* That page's formulation: **a perverse incentive predicts inefficiency, not failure.** Billable-hour law and fee-for-service medicine both carry the incentive and both work.

So the incentive alone is **not** the ratchet. *Proposed (Claude, open for Chris):* the incentive becomes a ratchet when **the discipline is missing**:
- **Competition / exit.** A rival who *cures* takes the whole treatment market. The incentive is capped wherever a competitor can collect the reward for solving the problem.
- **Attribution.** The principal can tell whose failure it was (see lag, counterfactual, repetition in the Ship of State page).

Where both are present you get inefficiency. Where both are gone (monopoly, consolidated jurisdiction, decades of lag) you get the inverted loop. This would explain why the government case is the worst case: it is the agent most able to foreclose exit and the one with the longest lag.

### Chris on the discipline condition (2026-09-24, round 4)

**IP removes the competition discipline.** Chris: *"this is usually addressed by those who want to see IP removed. If the company has the IP, there is no competition to keep things in check."*

**Insulin.** Chris: insulin was discovered decades ago, *"and yet even past IP, insulin makers continue to modify the formula in order for their IP to stay current."* He knows diabetics, and grants the new formulas *"do provide value due to ease of use and less side-effects, but that the market doesn't provide all levels of coverage might be the failure of IP."*
- *Grounding notes (Claude):* the source was animal **pancreas** (beef/pork), not blood; Banting's team sold the original patent to the University of Toronto for $1 each. Re-patenting incremental reformulations has a name, **evergreening**. On "all levels of coverage": old human insulins (Regular/NPH) are sold over the counter cheaply at some US pharmacies (Walmart ReliOn is the usual cited example), so the low tier exists but is unpromoted. And US insulin was reclassified as a *biologic* in 2020, which routes cheaper copies through the costly biosimilar pathway: **regulation as a second moat beside IP**. *To verify before leaning on it.*

**Chris's IP position: moderate, not abolitionist.** *"I am not completely against IP. I think copyright has its benefits for artists who make their living off of it, but I would agree we are erring on the side of too much protection."* The downside he names: *"companies being able to rent stuff that was normally purchased, and use it to prevent right to repair."* (This updates the earlier "IP set aside" note in [LZ course 03](../liquidzulu/course-03-homesteading.md).)

**The unifier, therapy = democracy.** Chris:
> *"I do think the therapy ideas and the democracy ideas are similar. It is hard on the 'customer' to know when they no longer need service!"*

*Claude, prior art:* this is the economist's **credence good** (Darby & Karni, 1973), a service whose quality or necessity the buyer can't judge *even after* consuming it, because **the seller is also the diagnostician**. The therapist, the doctor, the mechanic and the politician each tell you whether you still need them. So the attribution discipline sharpens into: **can the customer tell when they're done?** The electrician is only partly a credence good (the lights come on); therapy, chronic treatment and governing are almost fully credence goods.

**Revised discipline condition:** the incentive stays as mere inefficiency while **(1) a rival can collect the reward for solving the problem** (IP and regulatory moats remove this) and **(2) the customer can judge when the service is finished** (credence goods fail this). Government fails both, as usual.

## Prior art (check before claiming novelty)
- **The Shirky principle** (Clay Shirky, named by Kevin Kelly): *"institutions will try to preserve the problem to which they are the solution."* This is form 1, nearly verbatim.
- **Pournelle's Iron Law of Bureaucracy:** those devoted to the organization itself eventually control it. This supplies the *who*.
- **The cobra effect:** a bounty on a problem pays people to breed the problem (form 2).

What the vault would add over these: the **inversion** (form 3) and the **discipline condition** (why the incentive sometimes stays as inefficiency and sometimes becomes a ratchet).

## Open Questions
- Is the discipline condition right, and complete? Are there disciplined monopolies, or undisciplined competitive markets?
- Does medicine actually show treatments beating cures net of competition, or does the pattern come from regulation and third-party payment, meaning the discipline was removed rather than the incentive winning?
- Form 2 (creation) vs form 1 (preservation): is creation a different mechanism, or the same incentive with more reach?
