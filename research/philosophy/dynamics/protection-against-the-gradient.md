# Protection Against the Gradient — Defying Entropy Locally
> Chris's thesis: every equalizing force — markets, migration, competition — is a gradient that entropy eventually closes. Protection (borders, tariffs, moats, trust) is local order held against it. It always loses in the end, and holding it anyway is not irrational: it is what living things do.

## The thesis, in Chris's words
> "As a pessimist, entropy will always win… eventually it is gonna get ya, but you still want the local spots that go against it."

> "In theory entropy wins, so the idea of protectionism to hold back entropy is fine… this is the same thing as saying that humans still cling to life even though in the long run they are doomed… maybe the point here is **defying entropy is what life is all about**."

Three parts:
1. **The gradient always closes.** A difference in wages, prices or returns drives a flow until the difference is gone. The flow does not stop while any difference remains.
2. **Protection is local order against that flow.** It is never permanent and costs continuous effort, but defending it is rational *for those inside it*.
3. **We hop to the next pocket.** Coal → oil → renewables; computers → internet → AI. "Bubbles do pop and dynasties do end." Each pocket decays, and new ones form.

The physics underneath is already in [Symmetry Breaking](./symmetry-breaking.md) §Step 3: structure exists only because we are not at equilibrium. Low-entropy pockets (Prigogine's dissipative structures; Schrödinger's *life feeds on negative entropy*) persist only while energy is spent maintaining them. This page applies that to economics and politics.

## Mechanism 1 — the equalizing flow (borders as the worked case)
Chris's argument against [Bryan Caplan's open-borders thought experiment](../../debates/magpie-wallerstein-capitalism-critique.md#discussion):
- Remove the cost of moving, and migration continues from any below-average country to any above-average one.
- Labor supply rises where wages are high and falls where they are low, and wages converge. "Rinse repeat, this levels out. Entropy in action."
- If world output doubles from Chris's ~$12.5k per capita to ~$25k, **every country above the new average eventually loses relative to where it was.** "It is why protectionism exists."
- **Why Chris disagrees with Caplan:** real-world friction slows the process, but in principle entropy wins, so the protection is not irrational.

**The split inside the protected pocket (Claude's addition, accepted by Chris):** "the country loses" is really **its competing workers lose while its capital and land owners gain** (more workers per machine, acre and apartment). That predicts the coalitions: owners lean open, workers lean closed. Chris: "it has always been the 'elite' who are for immigration due to lower labor costs."

## Mechanism 2 — the last-round problem (the trust variant)
Repeatability makes trust work: cheating wins once and loses every future trade. But **"reputation is only good until it becomes more beneficial to erase it."** The pot you are trusted with grows alongside your reputation, so trust eventually funds its own betrayal.
- **EVE Online:** two documented candidates (Guinness):
  - **2006 EVE Intergalactic Bank:** "Cally" *founded and ran* it, then absconded with ~790B ISK of deposits (~$29k).
  - **2005 Guiding Hand Social Club:** a spy spent ~10 months becoming the CEO's right hand at Ubiqua Seraph, then looted every hangar (~30B ISK).
  
  Chris remembers "2006" and "stole every asset of his corporation". The date fits EIB; "rose inside and took everything" fits GHSC. Both are the same last-round pattern.
- **FTX:** Bankman-Fried "played the crypto markets for others until it became so large it was easier to steal."
- **Chris:** "Is this a flaw of capitalism? Probably no, it's a flaw in humans at best."

## Mechanism 1 meets Mechanism 2 — elite self-sabotage
Chris ties the two together. The winners of the open flow (owners buying cheaper labor) **extract until they erode their own base**, which is the last-round problem at the scale of a class:
- **Offshoring** cheapened production but **gutted the buyer base** that bought the products.
- **Importing labor** cut wage costs but **raised prices** (housing above all), so people "can no longer afford the products."

### Chris's grounding for the coalition claim — the party flip (2026-10-05)
> "In the 80s/90s it was actually US Republicans who were for immigration due to low wages… and Democrats were against it because they felt the low wages hurt the local workers. This seems to now be flipped, as Republicans now argue against immigration on the basis of cultural norms, and the argument is the 'elites' are now in the Democrat party."

Chris frames this as **his take on a live, charged issue with proponents on both sides**. If he is right, the owners-vs-workers split holds while the *parties* carrying it swapped, and the restrictionist case changed its basis from **wages to culture**. Grounded below.

**Net for the thesis:** the *economic* split (B, C, D-local) is well grounded, which is the gradient. ### Chris's closing read on the evidence (2026-10-05)
> "The largest study done on immigration was like a 7% increase in Florida, and it was hard to find wage [effects], but… 7% in Florida is nothing compared to the entire country, so of course competition didn't show a blip… it hasn't reached a point where it is large enough to measure countrywide. At best we see issues at the min-wage level with displacement… if businesses are using undocumented workers, the incentive is to hide this anyway! … **The Caplan experiment is enough for me to argue the worst case. That we are only seeing a fraction of this might show direction if not impact.**"

- **Claude's correction:** the Mariel boatlift was **7% of Miami's** labour force, not Florida's.
- **Claude's grounding:** Chris's dilution point is a recognised position, and it is **Borjas's** critique of local-area studies. A local shock spreads nationally as natives move away and capital moves in. That makes city comparisons *understate* the effect, which is why Borjas measures across national skill groups instead. The measurement problem is real, and the undocumented-labour data problem compounds it.
- **Position adopted:** argue from the frictionless worst case (Caplan's own thought experiment) for **direction**. The observed effects are a small, partly hidden fraction, so they can confirm the sign without settling the size.

## Friction is the rate, the gradient is the destination (Chris, 2026-10-05)
> "Caplan's experiment assumed no friction, which I agree is impossible, but that only showed what it gravitates to… friction will prevent complete evening out, and to be honest, the immigration guards are there to make this friction as high as possible. This is the same as the main contention of the civ-cycle: all we can do is hope to help slow the ratchet."

- **The frictionless model gives the attractor; friction sets the speed.** This is how Chris reconciles using Caplan for the worst case while calling it impossible. It also ties this page to [Civilizational Cycles](./civilizational-cycles/README.md): the end state is not avoidable, but the *rate* is a policy choice.
- **The same logic should discipline HOAs and unions**, and fails for the same reason:
  > "Without friction, the competition should keep them honest. They should have to prove their worth. Would I be better off keeping my dues and fixing everything myself? Very possibly. Unions should be free to enter/exit, and should have to also earn their dues… but we also see here friction that allows corruption."
- **Claude's synthesis, a partial answer to the open question below:** friction is *neutral*. It is a rate parameter that always favours whoever **currently holds the position**.
  - Against an *outside* gradient (migration, competition), the holder is the insider, so friction is protection.
  - Against the holder's *own members* (HOA board vs owners, union leadership vs workers), the holder is the incumbent manager, so friction is unaccountability.
  - Same mechanism, different counterparty. Whether friction is good depends on whose position it guards.
  - *(Union grounding: US right-to-work laws and Janus v. AFSCME (2018, public-sector agency fees) are exactly the "free to exit, must earn dues" lever. Claude, from memory, unverified.)*

## Open Questions
- **Is the *number* of protected pockets constant while each one decays?** This is Chris's "hop to the next pocket" against Wallerstein's perpetual cycle of making and breaking monopolies. If constant, what sets the number?
- **Which protections are worth their cost?** "Defying entropy is what life is about" licenses *trying*. It doesn't rank tariffs vs borders vs licensing by what each one costs to maintain against what it shields.
- **Friction cuts both ways.** Mobility friction is *protection* against an equalizing flow (borders, migration). The same friction *disables* competition as a discipline: HOAs as Tiebout "competing governments" fail because exit means moving house ([Chris's HOA specimen](../../debates/magpie-wallerstein-capitalism-critique.md#discussion)). Is there a general rule for when friction helps the insider and when it only shields the incumbent from accountability? *(Partly answered above: friction favours the current holder. Open: how do you tell, case by case, whose position is being guarded?)*
- **Does the trust variant have a cure short of serfdom?** Caps on how much any one person can take (segregated client funds, liability, clawbacks) shrink the last-round prize. Do they hold up at scale?
- Open gaps from the grounding pass: the Chamber of Commerce's position over time; party-split Gallup figures for the 1990s; Amiti et al. on consumer prices; Caplan (2019) text; Saiz & Wachter in detail.
